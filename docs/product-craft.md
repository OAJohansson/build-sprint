# Product craft

The people behind the playbook, what each one is known for, and where their ideas show up in my
process. Goal B is to work the way good product managers do; this page is the reading list and
the map. Their ideas are tools, not rules: at the scale of one product a day, use the lightest
version that works.

## What the brief is

The brief is a **one-page PRD** (product requirements document). The old style of PRD was a long
list of features written up front and handed to engineers; Cagan's criticism is that it decides
the solution before anyone has learned whether it works. The modern version, the shape most top
teams use (Lenny has collected many), is short and puts the *why* first:

1. Problem, who, and jobs: why this matters.
2. Alternatives and riskiest assumptions: what we don't know yet.
3. Success measures: how we'll know it worked.
4. MVP, requirements, no-gos and rabbit holes: what we'll build, and what we won't.

Requirements are written as behaviours the user can see, each with a check, not as designs.
That leaves room for the prototype to explore *how*, while everyone agrees on *what* and *why*.

## Where they disagree

- **How much to write down.** Cagan prefers prototypes to documents; Singer writes a careful pitch
  first; Pichler likes a one-page board. The brief is the middle way: one page, then prototype.
- **Planning vs discovering.** Pichler's roadmaps and strategy vs Torres's weekly continuous
  discovery. At one product a day, discovery wins; strategy matters across the 20 days.
- **Talking to users.** Torres and Fitzpatrick say real conversations every week; a simulated
  user is a cheap first pass, not a replacement.
