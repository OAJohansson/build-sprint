# Breaking unhealthy habits and handling lapses: evidence for a habit-tracker app

Evidence grades used below: **STRONG** = meta-analysis or several RCTs that agree; **MODERATE** = one or a few RCTs or well-designed experiments; **WEAK/POPULAR** = uncontrolled, company data, single small study, or popular claim. Researched October 2026; I fetched primary abstracts where I could and flag secondary-only figures.

## 1. Habit reversal training (HRT), competing responses and awareness training

### Takeaway
HRT (awareness training + a competing response that is physically incompatible with the habit + social support) is the best-supported behavioural treatment for body-focused repetitive behaviours (hair pulling, skin picking, nail biting), with large effects in trichotillomania (SMD about -1.1 to -1.2). STRONG for trichotillomania, MODERATE-to-WEAK for skin picking and nail biting, and WEAK for the step to everyday habits like snacking or scrolling. The parts that carry over are "notice the urge and its cue" plus "have a planned thing to do with your hands or body instead".

### Cited Findings
- A 2007 systematic review of blinded RCTs for trichotillomania found HRT had the largest effect (ES = -1.14, 95% CI -1.89 to -0.38), against clomipramine (-0.68) and SSRIs (0.02, no effect). Only 7 studies. — [Bloch et al. 2007 via QxMD](https://www.qxmd.com/r/17727824)
- McGuire et al. 2014 meta-analysis: behaviour therapy gives large effects for trichotillomania; SRIs a moderate pooled effect. — [PMC4163503](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4163503)
- An updated meta-analysis of 24 RCTs (26 comparisons, 857 participants) found a large benefit for behaviour therapy with HRT components over controls (SMD about -1.22, as reported in search summary). — [Frontiers / BFRB literature](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fnbeh.2019.00079/full) (note: the exact citation for the 24-RCT update was not opened directly; verify before quoting the number)
- Skin picking evidence is thin: a 2019 review found only 8 RCTs for trichotillomania and 2 for excoriation disorder. — [PMC6491945](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6491945)
- Real-world, uncontrolled therapist-delivered video HRT reported Hedges' g about 1.16 for excoriation disorder at weeks 14–16 (no control group). — [Psychiatrist.com, video HRT for BFRBs](https://www.psychiatrist.com/jcp/video-therapy-habit-reversal-training-body-focused-repetitive-behaviors/faq/)
- Self-help head-to-head RCT (Moritz group, 2022, N = 113, at least one BFRB): HRT (awareness + competing response) vs decoupling vs decoupling in sensu (imagined). Completion was highest for decoupling in sensu (68.6%) vs HRT (57.1%) and decoupling (53.5%); a secondary summary calls symptom differences mixed and small. — [Behavior Modification 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/01454455211010707); [BFRB.org summary](https://www.bfrb.org/research/a-head-to-head-comparison-of-three-self-help-techniques-to-reduce-body-focused-repetitive-behaviors)
- A 2012 skin-picking pilot found strong symptom decline with self-help HRT but not with decoupling; authors discouraged decoupling for skin picking. — [Macquarie, "Do-it-yourself" evaluation of self-help HRT vs decoupling](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/do-it-yourself-evaluation-of-self-help-habit-reversal-training-ve/)
- A six-week trial (N = 268, trichotillomania, nail or cheek biting) tested a "gentle touch" habit-replacement technique against waitlist; per news coverage, Moritz said roughly one-third to half of people with BFRBs benefit from decoupling (a quote, not a result). — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/stop-biting-nails-new-research-suggests-gentle-touch-help-rcna94956)

### Inferences
- The active ingredients that make sense in an app are: (a) **awareness**: logging the moment, place and feeling when the urge or habit happens (this is HRT's "awareness training"); (b) a **pre-chosen competing response** the user sets up per habit ("when I reach for my nails, I press my fingertips together for one minute"). A tracker can prompt for both at setup.
- Self-help HRT loses a lot of people (only about 57% completed in the Moritz trial), so keep the setup to one cue + one replacement action, not a long workbook.
- Generalising from BFRBs to scrolling or snacking is a plausible bet, not established fact; present it to users as "a technique from habit research", not as proven for their habit.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis found for HRT applied to non-clinical everyday habits (snacking, phone checking). Azrin & Nunn's 1973 original paper (very high reported success) was not retrieved; treat its claims as historical.
- No effect sizes found for nail biting specifically.

## 2. Urge surfing and mindfulness-based relapse prevention (MBRP); Brewer's app programmes

### Takeaway
Mindfulness approaches seem to change **how people respond to an urge** more than they reduce the urge itself. Evidence is MODERATE for MBRP as aftercare in substance use (lower heavy drinking and drug use at 12 months) and for brief urge-surfing instruction cutting cigarettes in the short term. But the large app RCT of Craving to Quit found **no quit-rate advantage** over an active control. So: a useful in-the-moment tool, not a stand-alone cure.

### Cited Findings
- Bowen & Marlatt 2009 "Surfing the urge" (Psychol Addict Behav 23(4):666–71): 123 undergraduate smokers randomised to brief mindfulness instructions or no instruction. Urges did **not** differ between groups, but the mindfulness group smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over 7 days. — [PubMed 20025372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20025372/). Popular press puts the reduction at about 37% by day 7 (secondary figure). — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-science-willpower/201111/how-mindfulness-makes-the-brain-immune-temptation)
- Bowen et al. 2014 JAMA Psychiatry RCT: 286 people after initial substance use treatment, randomised to MBRP, cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention (RP) or treatment-as-usual, followed 12 months. Both MBRP and RP lowered relapse risk vs usual care; MBRP's advantage was more durable at 12 months. Press figures at 1 year: drug use about 9% (MBRP) vs 14% (usual care) vs 17% (RP); heavy drinking about 8% (MBRP) vs about 20% in the other groups (secondary; verify in the paper). — [Bowen et al. 2014 PDF](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Article-1_Bowen-et-al-JAMA-2014.pdf); [drugfree.org](https://drugfree.org/?p=27776); [Recovery Answers](https://www.recoveryanswers.org/?p=260)
- Brewer et al. 2011 (N = 88): mindfulness training vs American Lung Association's Freedom From Smoking. CO-verified point-prevalence abstinence 36% vs 15% at end of treatment (trend), and 31% vs 6% at 17 weeks (significant). Small, single site. — [Einstein/Elsevier Pure](https://einstein.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/mindfulness-training-for-smoking-cessation-results-from-a-randomi/)
- Garrison et al. 2020 (Nicotine & Tobacco Research) Craving to Quit app RCT: 505 smokers, app-based mindfulness (245) vs experience-sampling control (260). Six-month quit rate 11.1% overall, no significant difference (9.8% app vs 12.1% control). The app may weaken the link between craving and smoking, but that did not translate into more abstinence. — [NAQC summary](https://www.naquitline.org/news/409678/Craving-to-Quit-A-Randomized-Controlled-Trial-of-Smartphone-app-based-Mindfulness-Training-for-Smok.htm); [ClinicalTrials NCT02134509](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02134509)

### Inferences
- The best fit for a tracker is a short "ride the urge" moment offered when the user logs an urge (not a slip): name the urge, notice it peak and fall, then log whether they acted. That also gives the app urge data separate from slip data.
- The Craving to Quit null result is a warning: app-delivered mindfulness alone, with an active control, did not beat simply tracking (experience sampling). Tracking itself may do much of the work.

### Gaps
- Unwinding Anxiety and Eat Right Now (Brewer's apps for anxiety and craving-related eating) were not verified this session. From memory there are small trials reporting reduced craving-related eating; treat as unverified.
- The 2022 Cochrane review on mindfulness for smoking cessation was not retrieved.

## 3. Friction and environmental restructuring

### Takeaway
Changing the cue and adding friction is among the most reliable levers for unwanted habits: habits survive a move only when the context stays the same (Wood), life changes open a window (Verplanken's habit discontinuity hypothesis), and the one sec app study shows a short pause with an easy "dismiss" option cut actual target-app openings by 57% over 6 weeks (uncontrolled field data, MODERATE). The **choice to back out** carried the effect more than the waiting itself.

### Cited Findings
- Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen 2023 PNAS (one sec: 10-second delay + deliberation message + option to dismiss before a target app opens). Field study: 719 new iOS users, 280 used it for all 6 weeks. Users closed the target app in about 36% of attempts (43% week 1, steady around 32–34% from week 3). Opening attempts fell 37% (166 → 105 per user per week); combined, actual openings fell 57%. Self-reported time on apps fell about 77 minutes/day (d = -0.56), problematic use fell (d = -0.70), satisfaction with use rose (d = 0.81). — [PMC9974409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409/)
- Same paper, controlled online experiment (N = 500, five conditions): the **option to dismiss** had the strongest effect; the full package did not beat option-only (p = .22); friction alone helped but added nothing beyond the option; the deliberation message alone had no effect. Authors suggest simplicity matters (cognitive overload). — [PMC9974409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409/)
- Limitations: no control group in the field study, self-selection, dropouts differed from completers, 6 weeks only, iOS only, app creator is a coauthor. — [PMC9974409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409/)
- The company claims a 57% reduction and better life satisfaction (marketing summary of the above). — [one-sec.app/research](https://one-sec.app/research/)
- Follow-up CHI 2024 longitudinal in-the-wild study of design frictions for smartphone overuse (Haliburton, Grüning et al.). — [LMU bib](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/haliburton2024chi/haliburton2024chi.bib)
- An RCT of a nudge package (10 strategies such as disabling non-essential notifications and greyscale) for problematic smartphone use exists. — [PMC9112639](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9112639)
- Wood, Tam & Guerrero Witt 2005: students' exercise, newspaper-reading and TV-watching habits survived the transfer to a new university only when aspects of the performance context stayed the same. — [Wood et al. 2005 PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Wood.Tam_.GuerreroWitt.2005_Changing_circumstances_disrupting_habits.pdf)
- Verplanken & Roy: habit discontinuity tested on travel-mode choice among university employees who had recently moved house vs not; a critique notes the sample was unrepresentative and the 12-month "recent move" cutoff arbitrary. — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/context-change-and-travel-mode-choice-combining-the-habit-discont/); [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/96712/)
- Walker, Thomas & Verplanken office-relocation field study: travel habit automaticity weakened right after the move, equally for those who did and did not change mode. — [Bath: "Old habits die hard"](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/old-habits-die-hard-travel-habit-formation-and-decay-during-an-of/)
- Verplanken & Wood: effective habit-change interventions disrupt the environmental cues that trigger the habit, ideally timed to natural life changes; disruption opens a window but does not by itself build a replacement. — [Bath: Interventions to break and create consumer habits](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/interventions-to-break-and-create-consumer-habits/); [Behavioral Policy: Healthy Habits](https://behavioralpolicy.org/?p=14397)
- A lab experiment (N = 84, arXiv 2026) found self-related cues (including a plain black screen) interrupted mindless short-form video watching; users preferred the subtle black screen. — [arXiv 2604.19424](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19424) (preprint, WEAK)

### Inferences
- The tracker should ask "what triggers it, and where?" and then suggest one concrete friction step (move the app off the home screen, keep snacks out of sight, leave cigarettes in the car). The app can't add OS-level friction itself, but it can help the user plan it.
- Offer a "fresh start" prompt around natural breaks (moving, new job, holiday, new month): the habit discontinuity research says these are when cues are weakest.
- The one sec result suggests the key design move is **a moment of choice with an easy exit**, not a long lecture: short message, one clear "not now" option.

### Gaps
- No controlled long-term (>6 months) study of digital friction tools found.
- Sizes of effects for physical friction on snacking (e.g. distance-to-food studies) were not retrieved this session.

## 4. Substitution vs suppression

### Takeaway
Trying not to think about something produces a small-to-moderate rebound afterwards (d about 0.30, STRONG-ish across meta-analyses), and the competing-response logic of HRT is built on replacement, not suppression. Evidence directly pitting "replace the habit" against "just stop" for everyday habits is thin, but the convergent evidence favours giving people something to do instead (MODERATE).

### Cited Findings
- Abramowitz, Tolin & Street 2001 meta-analysis (Clin Psychol Rev 21(5):683–703; about 28 controlled studies): small-to-moderate rebound effect of thought suppression, varying with the target thought and measurement method; clinical samples did not show larger rebounds. Average d = .30 is a secondary figure. — [Contextual Science record](https://contextualscience.org/publications/paradoxical_effects_thought_suppression_metaanalysis_controlled_studies); [Penn State course text](https://psu.pb.unizin.org/psych425/chapter/response-modulation-emotional-thought-suppression/)
- Wang, Hagger & Chatzisarantis meta-analysis (k = 31): rebound effects **after** suppression are consistently found, but immediate enhancement **during** suppression is largely absent. — [escholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0ps3k293)
- Wegner's ironic process theory has faced replication scrutiny. — [Atticus Li: replication crisis note](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/wegner-ironic-process/) (blog, WEAK)
- HRT's active element is a competing response (replacement), which gives the large BFRB effects in section 1. — [Moritz 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/01454455211010707)

### Inferences
- For each "stop" habit, the app should ask for a **replacement action** ("instead I will…") and let users log the replacement as a positive event. This avoids framing the whole experience as "don't think about X".
- Avoid copy that keeps naming the forbidden thing ("Don't scroll!") in reminders; name the replacement instead.

### Gaps
- No RCT found directly comparing "substitute behaviour" vs "abstain only" for snacking, phone use or alcohol in everyday adults.

## 5. Lapses: abstinence violation effect, what-the-hell effect, broken streaks

### Takeaway
A slip makes further slips more likely mainly through the **belief that you've failed**: dieters overeat after *thinking* they broke the diet (what-the-hell effect), and broken streaks shown in a log reduce later engagement even when actual past behaviour is the same (Silverman & Barasch 2023, MODERATE-STRONG, 7 studies). Interestingly, the classic abstinence violation effect (guilt and self-blame after a first lapse) did **not** predict relapse well in smoking diary studies; what predicted it was feeling like giving up, the size of the lapse, and repeat lapses.

### Cited Findings
- Silverman & Barasch 2023, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", JCR 49(6):1095–1117, 7 studies: intact streaks shown in behaviour logs raise later engagement vs broken streaks, **independent of actual past behaviour**: only how the log displays it matters. People treat the streak as a goal in itself. The effect is stronger when people blame themselves for the break and weaker when they can repair the streak. — [UDel repository](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160); [CU Boulder](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)
- Barasch (interview): messages that highlight a recent failure typically don't work; offer a new goal instead. — [CU Boulder news](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch)
- Polivy's "what-the-hell effect": dieters eat conservatively while they feel in control and counter-regulate (overeat) once they feel control is lost. A study manipulating the apparent size of a pizza slice (actual intake fixed) found restrained eaters who *thought* they'd overeaten ate more cookies afterwards: perceived violation, not actual intake, drives it. Polivy says it usually lasts for the day. — [Wikipedia: Counterregulatory eating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterregulatory_eating); [EFPSA blog](https://more.efpsa.org/rpblog/?p=1172); [U of T Magazine](https://magazine.utoronto.ca/?p=16585)
- Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto 2011 (203 smokers, electronic diaries): self-blame, self-efficacy and guilt after the first lapse did **not** predict relapse; responses to later, recurrent lapses mattered more. Efforts to reduce the AVE have generally failed to change clinical outcomes. — [PMC3296289](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296289)
- Shiffman et al. 1996 (133 participants): AVE indicators generally failed to predict progression to relapse, but feeling like giving up after the first lapse predicted a faster second lapse. — [PMC3790630](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3790630)
- Smoking more during the first lapse predicted higher risk of a second lapse (169 subjects). — [Psychopharmacology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00213-005-0175-4)
- Most single lapses in smoking progress to relapse fairly quickly; the first lapse is highly predictive of eventual failure. — [PMC4997809](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4997809)
- Duolingo: streaks called its most impactful feature, 9 million+ users with year-plus streaks, 600+ streak experiments, Streak Freeze lets users miss a day; none published as controlled studies (company/industry claims, WEAK). Some users report pressure or anxiety from streaks (small student study). — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks); [UKWMS thesis](https://repositori.ukwms.ac.id/47113/6/BAB%205.pdf)

### Inferences
- Streaks are a double-edged sword for a "stop" habit: an intact streak motivates, but **a streak counter that resets to 0 after one slip is close to the worst case** (self-blame + no repair + visible "failure" = the conditions under which Silverman & Barasch found the biggest drop). Prefer displays that survive a slip: "clean days this month: 24/27", "longest run", or a streak with repair ("get back on track tomorrow to keep it").
- The danger window is the **hours after a slip** (what-the-hell lasts "for the day"; quick progression to second lapse). The post-slip message is the single most important piece of copy in the app. Make it: normalise, separate the slip from the goal, and give one concrete next step for today or tomorrow.
- Since "feeling like giving up" predicted progression, the app could ask one quick question after a logged slip ("How do you feel about your goal right now?") and switch to a recovery flow if the answer is low.
- Log slip **size** (one cigarette vs a pack; 10 min vs 2 h): bigger first lapses predict more lapses, and logging size also avoids all-or-nothing scoring.

### Gaps
- Cochran & Tesser 1996 (goal-violation / what-the-hell in goal framing) was not found; cite only after checking.
- No peer-reviewed causal study of Duolingo's Streak Freeze or similar "repair" features found; Silverman & Barasch's repair moderator is the best evidence.

## 6. Self-compassion after failure; "never miss twice"; rewarding a return

### Takeaway
A kind, non-judgemental response to a slip reduces the spiral (Adams & Leary 2007, MODERATE) and the largest real-world behaviour-change study found that a tiny bonus for **coming back after a miss** was the single best of 50+ interventions (Milkman et al. 2021 Nature megastudy, +27% gym visits; STRONG design, but for building a habit, not stopping one). "Never miss twice" as a slogan has no direct trial, but fits both findings.

### Cited Findings
- Adams & Leary 2007 (J Soc Clin Psychol 26(10):1120–1144): restrained eaters who ate a doughnut and then got a self-compassion induction showed less distress and **ate less afterwards**, among highly restrictive eaters (i.e. self-compassion blocked the what-the-hell response). — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823)
- Breines & Chen 2012, "Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation", PSPB 38(9):1133–1143 (reference only retrieved). — [UNC reference list](https://cls.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3019/2016/05/ReferenceList_CLS_SelfCompassion_Sept19.16.pdf)
- Breines et al. 2014: on days with more appearance-related self-compassion, participants reported less disordered eating. — [self-compassion.org PDF](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/breineseatingdisorder.pdf)
- Milkman et al. 2021 Nature megastudy (61,000+ 24 Hour Fitness members, 53–54 interventions, 4 weeks): top performer gave 125 bonus points (about $0.09) for returning after a missed planned workout, raising visits by 27%. About 45% of interventions raised weekly visits by 9–27%; only about 8% produced lasting change after the 4 weeks. — [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits); [KVIA/CNN](https://kvia.com/?p=866811); [Scimex](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/which-programs-give-gym-goers-the-biggest-boost)

### Inferences
- After a slip, the app should (1) respond warmly and briefly, (2) explicitly frame the next day as the thing that counts ("one slip doesn't undo X clean days; the next day matters most"), and (3) **celebrate the return** visibly: a "comeback" mark on the day after a slip is the closest translation of the Milkman finding.
- Avoid guilt-based copy ("You broke your streak!") which echoes the self-blame condition where broken streaks hurt most.
- Effects in the megastudy mostly faded after the incentive ended, so don't count on in-app rewards to create lasting change by themselves.

### Gaps
- Breines & Chen's actual findings (from memory: self-compassion after a failure increased study time and motivation to make amends) were not verified this session.
- No trial of the specific "never miss twice" rule found; it's a popular heuristic (James Clear, Atomic Habits), WEAK.
- Neff's broader self-compassion research not retrieved.

## 7. Tracking abstinence days vs replacement behaviour; harm reduction vs all-or-nothing

### Takeaway
For smoking, cutting down first and quitting abruptly give the same quit rates (Cochrane 2019, RR 1.01, moderate certainty), so a gradual-reduction goal is a legitimate choice, not a second-best one (STRONG for smoking). Combined with the what-the-hell and streak findings, this favours goals and displays that measure **amount or frequency** and **replacements done**, rather than a single all-or-nothing "days clean" counter.

### Cited Findings
- Lindson et al. 2019 Cochrane review (CD013183): reduction-to-quit vs abrupt quitting RR 1.01 (95% CI 0.87–1.17; 22 studies, 9,219 participants; moderate certainty). Vs no treatment: RR 1.74 (0.90–3.38; very low certainty). Some evidence reduction-to-quit does better with varenicline as an aid. An earlier (withdrawn) version showed RR 0.77 favouring abrupt; don't cite that. — [Cochrane CD013183](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013183_can-people-stop-smoking-cutting-down-the-amount-they-smoke-first); [EBM summary](https://download.skyscape.com/download/ota/lonestar/B4562E59-87F6-47FA-8F09-D67E1FE536CA/ebmg.ed18/content/HTML/x/tl53.htm)
- The what-the-hell effect depends on the perceived violation of a rigid rule. — [Wikipedia: Counterregulatory eating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterregulatory_eating)
- Experience-sampling (tracking cravings and smoking) was as effective as an app teaching mindfulness in Craving to Quit (both about 10–12% quit at 6 months). — [NAQC](https://www.naquitline.org/news/409678/Craving-to-Quit-A-Randomized-Controlled-Trial-of-Smartphone-app-based-Mindfulness-Training-for-Smok.htm)

### Inferences
- Let users choose the goal type at setup: "quit completely" or "cut down to N per day/week". Both are evidence-backed for smoking; for scrolling or snacking, "cut down" is often the only realistic goal anyway.
- Show **trend** (this week vs last week, rolling 7-day total) as the main view, with clean days as a secondary stat. This keeps one slip from looking like total failure.
- Count replacement actions as wins ("swapped 5 times this week") so the app has positive events to show even in a bad week.

### Gaps
- No direct study found comparing "abstinence-day counter" vs "replacement-behaviour log" as app displays.
- Alcohol harm-reduction evidence (e.g. controlled-drinking goals, Dry January studies) not retrieved this session.

### Design implications in brief (synthesis across sections; inferences, not findings)
1. Setup: habit → main cue/place → one friction step → one replacement action → goal type (quit / cut down).
2. Logging: separate **urge resisted**, **replacement done** and **slip (with size)**. Urge logging is the awareness-training ingredient.
3. Display: forgiving progress (clean days this month, rolling trend, longest run) over a resettable streak; if a streak exists, allow repair.
4. After a slip: short, warm, no failure framing, one next step, ask how committed they feel; mark the next good day as a "comeback".
5. Optional in-the-moment tool: 60-second "ride the urge", plus a one sec-style "do you still want to?" pause with an easy exit.
