# Behaviour-change frameworks for a personal habit-tracker app (COM-B/BCW/BCTTv1, Fogg/Tiny Habits, SDT, identity-based habits)

Evidence labels used below: **STRONG** = multiple meta-analyses / large preregistered trials; **MODERATE** = single meta-analysis or several RCTs with heterogeneity; **WEAK** = small studies, correlational, or unreplicated; **POPULAR CLAIM** = practitioner/book claim without peer-reviewed outcome testing.

## How do COM-B and the BCW guide intervention design step by step, and which BCTs have the strongest meta-analytic evidence for physical activity, diet and habit formation?

### Takeaway
COM-B/BCW is a design *process* (diagnose Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, then pick intervention functions and BCTs); it is a well-adopted framework rather than a tested causal theory. Among BCTs, **self-monitoring of behaviour (BCTTv1 2.3) combined with goal setting (1.1) and other control-theory/self-regulation techniques (feedback on behaviour 2.2, review of goals 1.5, action planning 1.4)** has the most consistent meta-analytic support for physical activity and diet; effects are small (d ≈ 0.2–0.4) and shrink at long-term follow-up.

### Cited Findings
**COM-B / BCW / BCTTv1 structure (framework, not outcome evidence)**
- (From prior knowledge, not re-fetched this session; verify) COM-B: Behaviour arises from interacting Capability (physical, psychological), Opportunity (physical, social) and Motivation (reflective, automatic). The BCW (Michie, van Stralen & West 2011, *Implementation Science*) synthesised 19 frameworks into 9 intervention functions (education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modelling, enablement) and 7 policy categories. The BCW book (Michie, Atkins & West 2014) lays out 8 steps in 3 stages: (1) understand the behaviour — define the problem, select target behaviour, specify it, identify what needs to change (COM-B diagnosis); (2) identify intervention options — intervention functions, policy categories; (3) identify content and delivery — BCTs, mode of delivery. BCTTv1 (Michie et al. 2013, *Annals of Behavioral Medicine*) lists 93 BCTs in 16 hierarchical groups (e.g. 1 Goals and planning, 2 Feedback and monitoring, 8 Repetition and substitution, 7 Associations). — see Gaps (sources not fetched in this session)

**Michie et al. 2009 meta-regression (MODERATE–STRONG for self-monitoring)**
- 122 evaluations, N = 44,747 adults; healthy-eating and physical-activity interventions; pooled effect d = 0.31 (95% CI 0.26–0.36), I² = 69%. Content coded into 26 techniques (pre-BCTTv1 taxonomy). — [PubMed 19916637](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)
- "Self-monitoring" explained the most between-study heterogeneity (13%); interventions combining self-monitoring with ≥1 other control-theory technique (goal setting, feedback, review of goals) were more effective than others (d = 0.42 vs 0.26). — [PubMed 19916637](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)
- Per outcome: d = 0.32 for physical activity (I² = 58%) and 0.31 for healthy eating (I² = 73%). — [NCBI Bookshelf DARE abstract](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK77075/)

**Samdal et al. 2017 (IJBNPA) — overweight/obese adults, long-term (MODERATE)**
- 48 RCTs (≥12 weeks, 2007–2014, adults ≥40 y, mean BMI ≥30), 82 outcome reports. Short-term (≤6 mo) ES = 0.37; long-term (32 reports) ES = 0.24 (95% CI 0.15–0.33), I² = 59.4%. — [Samdal 2017, PMC5370453](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5370453/)
- **Goal setting (1.1) and self-monitoring of behaviour (2.3)** predicted effects at both short and long term; the number of BCTs unique to the intervention arm also predicted effect. **Feedback on outcome of behaviour (2.7), graded tasks (8.7) and adding objects to the environment (12.5, e.g. step counter)** predicted long-term effect. Some evidence that a patient-centred, autonomy-supportive counselling style mattered for maintenance (a link to SDT). — [Samdal 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5370453/)

**Howlett et al. 2019 (Translational Behavioral Medicine) — maintenance in inactive adults (MODERATE)**
- 26 RCTs (16 pooled) of healthy inactive adults, ≥6-month post-intervention follow-up. Post-intervention d = 0.32 (95% CI 0.16–0.48, n = 2,346); maintenance at ≥6 months d = 0.21 (0.12–0.30, n = 2,190). Fidelity rarely measured. — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10071272/)
- Which specific BCTs predicted maintenance in Howlett was not retrievable in this session (see Gaps).

**Habit-formation-specific intervention evidence**
- Lally et al. 2010: 96 volunteers each picked one eating/drinking/exercise behaviour to do daily in the same context for 84 days; automaticity followed an asymptotic curve; median time to plateau ≈ 66 days among those whose data fit the model, range 18–254 days (extrapolated). Sample was small and the finding has not been directly replicated. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [UCL news 2009](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [The Behavioral Scientist commentary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- Missing a single day did not materially derail the automaticity trajectory (Lally 2010) — reported in secondary sources; treat as WEAK. — [The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- "Ten Top Tips" (10TT) habit-based weight-loss leaflet: two-arm individually randomised trial in 14 English primary-care practices vs usual care, follow-up 3–24 months; planned n = 520; primary outcome weight loss at 3 months; secondary outcome automaticity. Published as Beeken et al. 2017, *Int J Obesity* 41(2):246–254. — [Protocol, PMC3490723](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3490723/); [ISRCTN16347068](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN16347068); [LSHTM record](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1300624). Outcome numbers not retrieved (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- For a habit app, the evidence-backed core loop is: **set a specific goal (1.1) → make an if-then action plan tied to a cue (1.4; 7.1 prompts/cues; 8.1 behavioural practice/rehearsal in a stable context) → log it (2.3) → show feedback on behaviour (2.2) → periodically review/adjust the goal (1.5, 1.7 review outcome goal) → graded progression (8.7).** That combination is the "self-monitoring + another control-theory technique" bundle that Michie 2009 found most effective.
- COM-B as product step: before building features, ask which component is failing for the target user. A habit tracker mostly addresses *reflective motivation* and *psychological capability* (planning, memory); prompts address *automatic motivation/physical opportunity*. If the user's barrier is opportunity (no time, no gym), logging alone won't help.
- Effect sizes are small and decay over time (0.31–0.37 short term → 0.21–0.24 long term). Product success metrics should expect modest behaviour change and track maintenance beyond the novelty window (e.g. week 8+ retention of logging), not just day-7.
- Automaticity takes weeks to months with high variance, so streak-based "21 days" messaging is not evidence-based; a "missed day doesn't reset progress" design is closer to Lally's finding.

### Gaps
- COM-B/BCW/BCTTv1 structural details (9 functions, 7 policy categories, 93 BCTs/16 groups, 8 steps) are from prior knowledge; primary sources (Michie, van Stralen & West 2011, *Implement Sci* 6:42; Michie et al. 2013 *Ann Behav Med* 46:81–95; bct-taxonomy.com) were not fetched this session.
- Dombrowski et al. (2012, obesity BCT review) not retrieved.
- Howlett 2019's BCT-level findings (which BCTs predicted maintenance) not retrieved; from memory they reported techniques such as action planning, instruction, prompts/cues, behaviour practice, graded tasks and self-reward as more frequent in effective maintenance interventions — **unverified**.
- Beeken 2017 10TT outcome figures (my recollection: small but significant additional weight loss at 3 months, and greater automaticity, with a non-significant difference at 24 months) could not be verified (PDF returned 403).
- No meta-analysis found isolating "habit formation" (automaticity as outcome) BCTs; Gardner et al. work on habit-based interventions would be the next lookup.

## Which BCTs are most commonly present or absent in commercial habit and health apps?

### Takeaway
Commercial apps contain few BCTs (typically ~3–7 per app), cluster around self-recording, feedback, information and social features, and almost never cite theory. Planning-related BCTs (action planning, implementation intentions, problem solving, review of goals) and habit-specific BCTs are largely absent — an opportunity for differentiation.

### Cited Findings
- **Crane et al. 2015 (JMIR), alcohol reduction apps:** of 800 store results (662 unique), 13.7% (91) were alcohol-reduction; 61 coded. Mean 3.56 BCTs per app (SD 3.39, median 2); 7 apps had none. Most common: facilitate self-recording 54.1%, information on consequences 42.6%, feedback on performance 41.0%, options for additional support 24.6%, direct to written materials 23.0%. No app mentioned theory; 16.4% mentioned evidence. Mentioning evidence (P = .02) and social support (P = .04) were associated with popularity; prompting review of goals associated with higher ratings (univariable only); environmental restructuring negatively associated with ratings. Authors call associations exploratory. — [Crane 2015, PMC4468601](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4468601/)
- **Conroy, Yang & Maher 2014 (AJPM), physical activity apps:** 167 top-ranked apps (Aug 2013) coded from store descriptions with CALO-RE; most descriptions included fewer than 4 BCTs; most common: instruction on how to perform, modelling/demonstration, performance feedback, goal setting, planning social support/change. Two app types emerged: educational and motivational. — [PSU Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/behavior-change-techniques-in-top-ranked-mobile-apps-for-physical/); [PSU news](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/mobile-health-apps-lack-behavior-change-techniques)
- **Yang, Maher & Conroy follow-up (in-app coding):** 100 top-ranked PA apps (Nov 2013); mean 6.6 BCTs per app; most taxonomy BCTs absent from every app; most common were social support, approval from others, instruction, demonstration, feedback. — [PSU Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/implementation-of-behavior-change-techniques-in-mobile-applicatio/)
- Middelweerd et al. 2014 (IJBNPA) review and content analysis of adult PA apps is a further source in the same vein. — [PMC4132213](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4132213/)

### Inferences
- Habit-tracker market leaders implement self-recording, feedback (charts/streaks), reminders and sometimes social features — matching the "commonly present" list. Under-supplied evidence-backed BCTs to differentiate on: **action planning/implementation intentions (1.4), problem solving/coping planning (1.2), review behaviour goals (1.5), graded tasks (8.7), habit formation (8.3: prompt rehearsal in the same context), restructuring the physical environment (12.1).**
- Crane's finding that "evidence mentioned" correlated with popularity suggests a lightweight "why this works" line may also help adoption (exploratory).

### Gaps
- Direito et al. 2014 (JMIR mHealth uHealth; PA and diet apps, CALO-RE coding) not retrieved — counts per app unverified.
- No content analysis found specifically of *habit-tracker* apps (e.g. Streaks, Habitica, Loop); this gap should be noted or searched separately (e.g. Stawarz, Cox & Blandford 2015 CHI "Beyond self-tracking and reminders" analysed habit apps and found they rarely support habit formation — **from memory, unverified**).

## What is the actual empirical support for the Fogg Behavior Model and Tiny Habits?

### Takeaway
The Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP) and Tiny Habits are **POPULAR CLAIMS / design heuristics** with very little peer-reviewed outcome evidence. No RCT of the Tiny Habits method itself was found. The components that *do* have evidence come from other literatures: making behaviours small/easy (graded tasks), anchoring to an existing routine (implementation intentions; context-dependent repetition), and prompts/cues.

### Cited Findings
- Search for a Tiny Habits weight-loss RCT returned only popular coverage; the book's evidence claim is ~20 years of research and coaching of 40,000+ people (coaching data, not trials); Fogg's own weight loss is anecdotal. — [WRVO / NPR coverage](https://www.wrvo.org/health/2016-08-13/how-to-make-healthy-life-changes-from-tiny-habits); [Bokus book listing](https://www.bokus.com/bok/9780753553251/tiny-habits); [WeightWatchers blog](https://www.weightwatchers.com/ca/en/blog/how-change-your-habits-4-easy-steps-success)
- Academic engagement is mostly applications and simulations, not tests: a 2018 ADCAIJ paper models the "action line" with agent-based simulation (no human participants); a 2025 BMC Public Health scoping review (Anselmi et al.) maps how FBM is used in interventions (findings not retrieved). — [Gredos USAL](https://gredos.usal.es/xmlui/handle/10366/138393); [UOC record of scoping review](https://recerca.uoc.edu/documentos/68fd59b25170e849fda94457?lang=en)
- Critiques (non-peer-reviewed) note FBM collapses motivation into one dimension and is less diagnostic than COM-B. — [Thinking About Behavior Substack](https://thinkingaboutbehavior.substack.com/p/comparing-fogg-behavior-model-and)
- The adjacent, evidenced habit-based approach is the UCL "Ten Top Tips"/habit-advice line (randomised primary-care trial; Beeken 2017) — similar ingredients (small consistent actions, same context) but not Fogg's method. — [10TT protocol](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3490723/)

### Inferences
- Treat B = MAP as a design checklist (is there a prompt? is the action easy enough? is motivation present right now?) — it maps cleanly onto COM-B (Ability ≈ Capability + some Opportunity; Prompt ≈ cue/opportunity; Motivation ≈ Motivation).
- Defensible product choices drawn from Tiny Habits *because they also have support elsewhere*: "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]" recipe = implementation intention + stable context cue; starting tiny = graded tasks (8.7, predicted long-term effects in Samdal 2017).
- "Celebration / Shine" (self-generated positive emotion immediately after the behaviour) is untested as such. Closest evidenced analogues: positive feedback raises intrinsic motivation (d ≈ 0.33, Deci et al. 1999) and self-reward (BCT 10.9) — but no direct trial of celebration on habit automaticity was found. Present it as optional, not as "science says".

### Gaps
- No RCT, quasi-experiment or published outcome dataset of Tiny Habits found. Fogg has published mainly on persuasive technology (e.g. 2009 Persuasive conference paper introducing FBM) — not outcome trials.
- Full findings of the 2025 FBM scoping review not retrieved.
- No study found testing celebration/"shine" on automaticity.

## Self-Determination Theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness in apps; intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation; when rewards/gamification undermine motivation

### Takeaway
SDT has **STRONG** support as a correlational/experimental theory and **MODERATE** support for SDT-based health interventions (small effects, mediated by autonomous motivation and perceived need support). Expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards reliably undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation in lab studies (d ≈ −0.3 to −0.4), while informational positive feedback enhances it (d ≈ +0.3). Gamification shows a small–medium short-term effect on physical activity that shrinks after the intervention.

### Cited Findings
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (Psych Bulletin), 128 experiments:** engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); tangible and expected rewards also reduced it; effects on self-reported interest smaller (d = −0.15, −0.17). Positive (verbal) feedback enhanced free-choice behaviour (d = +0.33) and interest (d = +0.31). Tangible rewards more detrimental for children than college students. Contested by Cameron & Pierce (1994 and later), who argued rewards overall do not decrease intrinsic motivation. — [Deci et al. 1999 PDF (UW)](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001 follow-up](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- **Ntoumanis et al. 2021 (Health Psychology Review), 73 experimental SDT-informed health interventions:** small-to-medium improvements in SDT constructs and health behaviour (end of intervention and follow-up); small gains in physical and psychological health. Increases in perceived need support and autonomous motivation (not controlled motivation or amotivation) were associated with behaviour change. Authors describe effects as modest and heterogeneous. — [Birmingham research portal](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-self-determination-theory-informed-interventio/); [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/3827204)
- Sheeran et al. (RCT meta-analysis of SDT interventions, Ntoumanis co-author): overall d+ = 0.23 across 65 tests; autonomous motivation and perceived competence mediated effects. — reported in [Ntoumanis search summary / Birmingham portal](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/self-determination-theory-interventions-for-health-behavior-chang/) (secondary; verify)
- **Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006 (Motivation and Emotion 30:347–363), 4 studies:** in-game autonomy and competence predicted enjoyment, preference and pre–post well-being; intuitive controls related to competence; in an MMO community, autonomy, competence and relatedness each independently predicted enjoyment and future play. — [Rochester PDF](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf); [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/7228/)
- **Mazeas et al. 2022 (JMIR), gamification and physical activity:** 16 RCTs, n = 2,407; g = 0.42 (95% CI 0.14–0.69); +1,610 steps/day (95% CI 372–2,847); MVPA effect non-significant (g = 0.31, CI −0.19 to 0.80); post-intervention follow-up (~14 weeks) g = 0.15 (0.07–0.23). Authors call for more rigorous trials. — [Mazeas 2022, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2022/1/E26779/)
- Samdal 2017 noted autonomy-supportive, patient-centred style seemed important for long-term maintenance. — [Samdal 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5370453/)
- A 2024 review of SDT in behaviour-change technologies exists for design mapping. — [arXiv 2402.00121](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.00121)

### Inferences
- **Autonomy:** let the user choose their own habits, schedules, wording and reminder times; avoid controlling language ("You must", guilt notifications); offer rationale rather than commands; allow pausing/skipping without penalty.
- **Competence:** start small with graded progression; informational feedback ("4 of 5 this week") rather than evaluative/controlling feedback; show progress trends rather than only an all-or-nothing streak that breaks.
- **Relatedness:** optional, opt-in sharing/accountability partner; a solo personal tracker can still offer warmth in tone. Crane 2015 found social support associated with popularity (exploratory).
- **Rewards/gamification:** expected, contingent tangible-style rewards (points, badges for doing the task) risk undermining intrinsic motivation when the behaviour is already interesting, and gamification effects fade after removal (g 0.42 → 0.15). Prefer informational feedback and unexpected acknowledgement; if using streaks/badges, frame them as information about consistency, and design for the "after the novelty" phase. Overjustification risk is lowest for behaviours with low initial intrinsic interest (inference from the reward literature; check).

### Gaps
- Original Sheeran et al. SDT meta-analysis citation and full figures not fetched.
- No meta-analysis found specifically on streak mechanics or loss-aversion streaks in habit apps; evidence on "streak broken" demotivation (what-the-hell effect) would need a separate search.
- Cameron/Pierce counter-meta-analyses not fetched; the debate is noted but not detailed.

## Identity-based habits: does framing behaviour as identity ("I am a runner") help?

### Takeaway
James Clear's identity-based habits are a **POPULAR CLAIM**. The headline academic support — Bryan et al. 2011 noun ("be a voter") vs verb ("vote") — came from small samples and **failed to replicate** in large preregistered field experiments (Gerber, Huber & Fang). Correlational work (Verplanken & Sui 2019) shows habits and identity are linked, and Oyserman's identity-based motivation theory suggests identity framing helps when the identity feels congruent and the difficulty is interpreted as meaningful — but causal evidence that identity labels build habits is **WEAK**.

### Cited Findings
- **Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck 2011 (PNAS), 3 experiments:** Exp 1 (California, N = 34 unregistered): "very/extremely interested" in registering 87.5% noun vs 55.6% verb. Exp 2 (California 2008, N = 88): turnout 95.5% vs 81.8%. Exp 3 (New Jersey 2009, N = 214): turnout 89.9% vs 79.0%. Effects of 11–14 points from small samples. — [Bryan et al. 2011, PMC3150938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3150938)
- **Gerber, Huber & Fang (2017/2018, Political Psychology 39):** new replication experiment testing whether noun priming has "outsized" turnout effects — summary describes the original 11–14-point claim as from two small field experiments. — [Yale ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/resource/do-subtle-linguistic-interventions-priming-a-social-identity-as-a-voter-have-outsized); [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/pops.12446/)
- **Gerber, Huber & Fang (Behavioural Public Policy, online 2020; vol 7, 2023):** large-scale preregistered replication in the 2016 US presidential election found noun wording did not increase turnout vs verb wording, even in competitive states; notes two subsequent large-scale replications also found no effect. — [Cambridge, BPP](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/abs/voting-behavior-is-unaffected-by-subtle-linguistic-cues-evidence-from-a-psychologically-authentic-replication/7CA9BE69C3213D5030A2BD11A5C53450); [J-PAL copy](https://povertyactionlab.org/media/file-research-paper/voting-behavior-unaffected-subtle-linguistic-cues-evidence); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/bpubpo/v7y2023i2p380-394_8.html)
- **Verplanken & Sui 2019 (Frontiers in Psychology 10:1504), 2 studies:** across 80 behaviours reflecting 10 basic values, self-reported habit strength correlated with "true self" ratings; the habit–identity link was stronger with cognitive self-integration, self-esteem and ideal-self orientation (Study 1); a value-affirmation manipulation strengthened habit–identity links (Study 2). Authors suggest linking habits to identity/values may sustain new behaviours (a proposal, not a tested intervention). — [Verplanken & Sui 2019, PMC6635880](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6635880/)

### Inferences
- Do not market "identity framing" as proven. A safer evidence-informed version: let users optionally attach a **value or reason** ("why this matters to me") to each habit — value affirmation strengthened habit–identity links in Verplanken & Sui Study 2, and it fits SDT's autonomous motivation (identified regulation).
- Avoid one-shot noun-label nudges as a core mechanism; they are the specific manipulation that failed to replicate.
- Identity is plausibly an *outcome* of repetition (habits become self-defining over time) rather than a lever that produces it; a gentle "you've done this 30 times — this is part of your routine" reflection matches that direction without overclaiming.

### Gaps
- Oyserman's identity-based motivation (IBM) theory and its intervention trials (e.g. school-to-jobs studies; "difficulty as importance" framing) were not retrieved this session — claims about IBM above are from prior knowledge and should be verified (Oyserman 2009 *J Consumer Psychology*; Oyserman, Bybee & Terry 2006 *JPSP*).
- No RCT found testing "I am a [noun]" identity framing on health habit formation or automaticity.
- Exact effect estimates from the Gerber et al. replications were not extracted.
- James Clear's *Atomic Habits* identity framing has no peer-reviewed test; it is a synthesis/popular claim.
