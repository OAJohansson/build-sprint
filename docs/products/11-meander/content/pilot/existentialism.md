# Existentialism: content pilot

> For review only: the text, not the design. Every claim was graded against the cited source. About 1666 words, plus the thinkers (unchanged).

*Philosophy that starts from the single person who must choose how to live. <sup>[1][2][3]</sup>*

## In one minute

Existentialism is a loose family of ideas, not a tidy school. It starts from concrete, first-person life rather than a detached 'view from nowhere'. A central idea: no fixed nature is handed to us, so we make ourselves through our choices and are responsible for who we become. Kierkegaard and Nietzsche laid the groundwork in the nineteenth century; Sartre and Beauvoir made it famous in mid-twentieth-century France. <sup>[1][4]</sup>

It matters because it takes on questions we all meet: how to live when no rulebook tells us, how to stop drifting with the crowd, and how to face anxiety, death and meaninglessness. Its ideas spread into novels, films, theology, psychotherapy and radical, emancipatory politics. And it is not moral nihilism: a good life is possible, one where we own our choices and help others be free too. <sup>[1]</sup>

## It asks

1. If nothing hands me a purpose, how do I live? <sup>[5][6][7]</sup>
2. Am I living my own life, or just doing what everyone else does? <sup>[8]</sup>
3. What do I do with the anxiety of being free to choose? <sup>[3][9]</sup>

## Themes

### Existence before essence

*We have no fixed nature; we make ourselves through what we choose.*

Sartre called it 'the first principle of existentialism': existence precedes essence, an idea he adapted from Heidegger. No built-in human nature decides who we are. A person first 'turns up' and only afterwards defines himself, through choices made as life unfolds. Hard facts still limit us: our body, desires and historical setting, which existentialists call 'facticity'. But we can question those facts, give them meaning and decide what to do with them. That power is 'transcendence'. <sup>[10]</sup>

Thinkers: Jean-Paul Sartre

### Freedom and anxiety

*Being free means I alone answer for my choices, and that feels dizzying.*

If nothing fixed decides who we are, we are free and responsible for what we make of ourselves. Sartre's coward is not made that way by childhood or genes; he 'makes himself a coward' by his decisions. Seeing this brings anxiety, because no divine will or moral absolute can choose for us or excuse us: there are 'no excuses behind us nor justifications before us'. Kierkegaard called anxiety 'the dizziness of freedom'. Existentialists ask us to face it, as a mood that can shake us awake. <sup>[2][3][11][9]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Jean-Paul Sartre

### Authenticity versus the crowd

*Most of the time we drift with 'them'; authenticity means owning our life.*

Existentialists criticise living by borrowed norms. Kierkegaard wrote of 'the public', Nietzsche of 'the herd' and Heidegger of 'the They'. As Heidegger put it, we enjoy, read and judge things as 'they' do. Conforming is comforting, because doing what everyone does feels like living well, but it hides our freedom from us. Sartre and Beauvoir call this self-deception 'bad faith'. I am in bad faith when I treat myself as a fixed thing ('I am what I am'), and also when I pretend my situation does not limit my choices. <sup>[5][8][12]</sup>

What authenticity looks like varies. For Kierkegaard it is a passionate commitment that pulls a scattered life together. For Nietzsche it is saying yes to one's whole life, even to living it again forever. For Heidegger it is 'resoluteness', a focused grip on one's possibilities that stays open to letting them go. For Sartre and Beauvoir it is clear-eyed acceptance of our unstable situation and responsibility for what we do. <sup>[13][14][15][12]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir

### Meaning without guarantees

*Without God or a built-in purpose, meaning has to be made, not found.*

As nineteenth-century Europe grew secular and scientific, the religious frame that gave life moral direction began to collapse. Nietzsche diagnosed the result, nihilism, with his image of the death of God. Camus named the clash between our 'wild longing for clarity' and 'the unreasonable silence of the world' the absurd. Yet this need not end in despair. For Camus the absurd calls for action, not suicide. Beauvoir held that existence is not absurd but ambiguous: its meaning is never fixed, so we keep making it. <sup>[1][5][16][17][18][12]</sup>

Thinkers: Friedrich Nietzsche, Albert Camus, Simone de Beauvoir

### My freedom needs yours

*Other people can trap us as objects, yet my freedom depends on theirs.*

Sartre argued that we see ourselves through 'the look' of others, whose gaze can turn us into objects. Relationships become a power struggle, summed up in his play No Exit: 'Hell is—other people.' But there is a better way. Beauvoir's 'authentic love' nurtures the other person's freedom instead of possessing them. She and Sartre concluded that wanting my own freedom commits me to wanting it for others. In Beauvoir's words, to will oneself free is also to will others free. For Sartre, the Nazi occupation, his time as a prisoner of war and his Marxist critics then shifted the focus from the individual to the social. <sup>[19][6][20][21]</sup>

Thinkers: Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir

### Faith and the leap

*For religious existentialists, faith is a personal commitment reason can't justify.*

Existentialism is not only atheist. Kierkegaard, a Christian, prized 'a truth which is true for me', lived and felt rather than proved. In Fear and Trembling, Abraham is told by God to sacrifice his son Isaac. He becomes a 'knight of faith' by leaping into a paradox reason cannot explain, alone with a choice no one else understands. The Jewish existentialist Martin Buber stressed meeting another person as a 'You', not an 'It'. Nietzsche and Sartre, by contrast, saw God as irrelevant or harmful to our deepest choices. <sup>[1][22][13][23][24]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jean-Paul Sartre

## Where it came from

Hints of an 'existential attitude' go back to the Stoics, Augustine, Montaigne and Pascal. But the ideas became a movement only in the nineteenth century, as a secular, scientific world view replaced the religious frame that gave life direction, and industry and bureaucracy made society feel impersonal. Kierkegaard attacked Hegel's grand system for leaving out the single existing person; Nietzsche diagnosed the coming nihilism. Novelists counted too: Dostoevsky's 'underground man' rebels against being reduced to 'a piano key'. <sup>[5][22][3][25]</sup>

In the twentieth century, German thinkers such as Husserl, Jaspers and Heidegger laid more groundwork, and phenomenology, the careful description of everyday experience, gave the movement its roots. Heidegger's Being and Time (1927) shaped Sartre and Beauvoir. The movement then exploded in mid-century France, against the backdrop of the Second World War, the death camps and the atomic bomb. Sartre's Being and Nothingness appeared in 1943, the year Gabriel Marcel coined the word 'existentialism'. <sup>[1][26][27][28][29]</sup>

Who counts as an existentialist is disputed. The term names no single doctrine, and most thinkers grouped under it never used it or rejected it; only Sartre and Beauvoir openly claimed it. Heidegger repudiated Sartre's existentialism in his 'Letter on Humanism' (1947), yet both encyclopedias still count him among its key figures. Camus gave a 1945 interview titled, in English, 'No, I am not an existentialist', though the SEP places his Myth of Sisyphus squarely in the tradition. <sup>[30][31][1][18][7]</sup>

## Key works

- **Fear and Trembling** (Søren Kierkegaard, 1843): Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac, used to ask what faith demands beyond ethics. <sup>[32][13][33]</sup>
- **The Gay Science** (Friedrich Nietzsche, 1882; expanded 1887): Poses the eternal-recurrence test: could you will your life again, forever? <sup>[34][14]</sup>
- **Being and Time** (Martin Heidegger, 1927): Human existence as being-in-the-world, lost in 'the They' until anxiety calls it back. <sup>[27][26][15]</sup>
- **The Myth of Sisyphus** (Albert Camus, 1942): Asks whether life is worth living, through Sisyphus forever pushing his rock. <sup>[35][36]</sup>
- **Being and Nothingness** (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1943): The movement's defining treatise: radical freedom, bad faith and the look. <sup>[37][38][39]</sup>
- **Existentialism Is a Humanism** (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1945 lecture, published 1946): A packed Paris lecture, an accessible version of Being and Nothingness. <sup>[28][40]</sup>
- **The Second Sex** (Simone de Beauvoir, 1949): How women are made man's 'Other'; a founding text of second-wave feminism. <sup>[41][20]</sup>

## Main criticisms

In Germany, Adorno's The Jargon of Authenticity called existentialism, and Heidegger above all, obscure, abstract and even mystical; many analytic philosophers agreed. After Marxist criticism in the 1940s and 1950s, Sartre himself decided his early 'radical' freedom was too abstract and ignored the social conditions that limit real choices. Merleau-Ponty objected that no one is free in isolation from others and history. Later, French structuralists faulted Sartre for neglecting language and impersonal structures of meaning. <sup>[42][43][20]</sup>

Other criticisms are moral and political. Existentialists were accused of neglecting social and political structures, with dangerous results; Heidegger remains notorious for his involvement with National Socialism. Nietzsche's ideal of the self-creating individual is openly elitist and anti-democratic, and Nazi ideologues exploited his work, in a way the IEP calls 'utterly misdirected'. Camus accused Kierkegaard, Jaspers and Heidegger of escaping into 'forced hope'. And some argue the label does more harm than good, lumping very different ideas together. <sup>[42][31][14][44][16][30]</sup>

## Why it still matters

Existentialism reached far beyond philosophy: into films by Bergman and Godard, the theatre of the absurd and Beckett, and painting from Munch to Hopper. Its stress on freedom fed the politics of Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X and the writing of Richard Wright and Ralph Ellison. Beauvoir laid foundations for second-wave feminism, and Fanon's Black Skin, White Masks drew on her, Sartre and Merleau-Ponty. It also inspired existential psychotherapy, from Viktor Frankl to Rollo May and Irvin Yalom. <sup>[1][25][45]</sup>

Within academic philosophy the verdicts differ. The IEP judges that the movement as a whole has had relatively little direct influence there, though Nietzsche and Heidegger remain very much alive. The SEP sees it thriving: it shaped Foucault and Derrida, theories of the self as a life story, and cognitive science's picture of the embodied mind. Its focus on emotion opened a whole field of research, and it insisted philosophy deal directly with real-life topics such as sex and death. It has also shaped medicine and nursing, and its courses remain among philosophy's most popular. <sup>[42][46][47][48][49][50][51]</sup>

## Study this school

> Teach me Existentialism as a short Socratic module. Start with what it is: a loose movement that begins from concrete human existence, rooted in Kierkegaard and Nietzsche and made famous by Sartre and Beauvoir in mid-20th-century France. Then cover its six themes: existence before essence; freedom and anxiety; authenticity versus the crowd; meaning without guarantees; my freedom needs yours; faith and the leap. Note that Camus and Heidegger rejected the label. Ask me one question at a time and finish with a five-question quiz. <sup>[1][10][3][8][5][6][13][7][31]</sup>
