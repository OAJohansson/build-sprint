# Existentialism: content pilot

> For review only: the text, not the design. Every claim was graded against the cited source. About 2573 words, plus the thinkers (unchanged).

*Philosophy that starts from the single person who must choose how to live. <sup>[1][2][3]</sup>*

## In one minute

Existentialism is a loose family of ideas, not a tidy school. It starts from life as a person lives it, not from a detached viewpoint. Its central idea is that we are not born with a fixed nature. We make ourselves through our choices, and we are responsible for who we become. Two nineteenth-century thinkers laid the groundwork: Kierkegaard, from Denmark, and Nietzsche, a German philosopher. In mid-twentieth-century France, Sartre and Beauvoir made it famous. <sup>[1][4][5][6]</sup>

It rejects fixed moral rules, but it does not say that morality is empty. A good life is possible: one where we own our choices and help others be free too. Above all, it takes on questions we all meet: <sup>[1]</sup>

- How do I live when no rulebook tells me how?
- How do I stop drifting along with the crowd?
- How do I face anxiety, death and a sense that life is meaningless?

## It asks

1. If nothing hands me a purpose, how do I live? <sup>[7][8][9]</sup>
2. Am I living my own life, or just doing what everyone else does? <sup>[10]</sup>
3. What do I do with the anxiety of being free to choose? <sup>[3][11]</sup>

## Themes

### Existence before essence

*We are not born with a fixed nature. We make ourselves through what we choose.*

Sartre called it 'the first principle of existentialism': existence precedes essence. Your 'essence' would be a fixed nature that decides who you are. Existentialists say there is none. A person first 'turns up' and only afterwards defines himself, through choices made as life unfolds. Sartre adapted the idea from Heidegger, a German philosopher. Hard facts still limit us, such as our body and the time and place we live in. Existentialists call these facts our 'facticity'. But we can question them, give them meaning and decide what to do with them. This power to rise above the facts is called 'transcendence'. <sup>[12][1]</sup>

Thinkers: Jean-Paul Sartre

### Freedom and anxiety

*Being free means I alone answer for my choices, and that feels dizzying.*

If nothing fixed decides who we are, then we are free. We are also responsible for what we make of ourselves. Take Sartre's example of a coward. Childhood or genes did not make him that way; he 'makes himself a coward' by his decisions. Seeing this brings anxiety. No god and no absolute moral rule can choose for us or excuse us. In Sartre's words, there are 'no excuses behind us nor justifications before us'. Kierkegaard called anxiety 'the dizziness of freedom'. Existentialists ask us to face this feeling, not run from it. It can shake us out of our comfortable routines. <sup>[2][3][13][11]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Jean-Paul Sartre

### Authenticity versus the crowd

*Most of the time we just do what everyone else does. Being authentic means living our own life.*

Being authentic means being true to yourself. Existentialists criticise living by rules and expectations we pick up from others. As Heidegger put it, we enjoy, read and judge things as 'they' do. Doing what everyone does is comforting, because it feels like living well. But it hides our freedom from us. Sartre and Beauvoir call this self-deception 'bad faith'. I am in bad faith when I treat myself as a fixed thing: 'I am what I am'. I am also in bad faith when I pretend my situation puts no limits on my choices. Several thinkers had a name for the crowd we drift with: <sup>[7][10][14][1]</sup>

- Kierkegaard: 'the public'
- Nietzsche: 'the herd'
- Heidegger: 'the They'

What authenticity looks like varies by thinker: <sup>[15][16][17][14]</sup>

- Kierkegaard: a passionate commitment that pulls a scattered life together.
- Nietzsche: saying yes to your whole life, even to living it again forever.
- Heidegger: 'resoluteness', a focused grip on your chosen path that stays open to letting it go.
- Sartre and Beauvoir: seeing your unsettled situation clearly, accepting it, and taking responsibility for what you do.

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir

### Meaning without guarantees

*Without God or a built-in purpose, meaning has to be made, not found.*

As nineteenth-century Europe became less religious and more scientific, the old religious picture that gave life moral direction began to collapse. Nietzsche diagnosed the result: nihilism, the shocking sense that our existence has no overall reason or purpose. His image for this loss was 'the death of God'. The French novelist Albert Camus called our situation 'the absurd'. It is the clash between our 'wild longing for clarity' and 'the unreasonable silence of the world'. Yet this need not end in despair. For Camus, the absurd calls for action, not suicide. Beauvoir held that existence is not absurd but ambiguous: its meaning is never fixed, so we keep making it. <sup>[1][7][18][19][20][14][21]</sup>

Thinkers: Friedrich Nietzsche, Albert Camus, Simone de Beauvoir

### My freedom needs yours

*Other people can trap us as objects, yet my freedom depends on theirs.*

Sartre and Beauvoir argued that we see ourselves through 'the look' of others. Their gaze can turn us into objects. Relationships then become a power struggle. Sartre's play No Exit sums this up in its famous line that hell is other people. But there is a better way. Beauvoir's 'authentic love' nurtures the other person's freedom instead of possessing them. She and Sartre concluded that wanting my own freedom commits me to wanting it for others. In Beauvoir's words, to will oneself free is also to will others free. Later, Sartre shifted his focus from the single person to society. Three things pushed him: <sup>[22][8][23][24]</sup>

- the Nazi occupation of France
- his own time as a prisoner of war
- attacks on his ideas from Marxist critics

Thinkers: Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir

### Faith and the leap

*For religious existentialists, faith is a personal commitment reason can't justify.*

Existentialism is not only atheist. Kierkegaard was a Christian. He prized 'a truth which is true for me', lived and felt rather than proved. His book Fear and Trembling retells the Bible story in which God tells Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac. Abraham becomes a 'knight of faith': someone who commits to a truth that reason cannot make sense of. He 'leaps' into a paradox, alone with a choice no one else understands. The Jewish thinker Martin Buber stressed meeting another person as a 'You', a whole person. The opposite is treating them as an 'It', a thing to use. Nietzsche and Sartre, by contrast, saw God as irrelevant or even harmful to our deepest choices. <sup>[1][25][15][26][27][28]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jean-Paul Sartre

## Where it came from

Early hints of this outlook go back to ancient philosophy and to later writers on death and the meaning of life. But the ideas became a movement only in the nineteenth century. Religion's hold was fading, and industry and bureaucracy made society feel impersonal. Early voices included: <sup>[7][25][3][29][1]</sup>

- Kierkegaard, who attacked the philosopher Hegel's system of all reality for leaving out the single living person.
- Nietzsche, who foresaw the coming nihilism.
- The Russian novelist Dostoevsky, whose 'underground man' refuses to be reduced to 'a piano key' in a world run by fixed laws.

In the twentieth century, German thinkers such as Husserl, Jaspers and Heidegger laid more groundwork. They gave the movement its roots in phenomenology: the study of how we experience the world from our own point of view. Heidegger's book Being and Time (1927) shaped Sartre and Beauvoir. The movement then exploded in France in the middle of the century. Sartre's Being and Nothingness appeared in 1943, the year the French thinker Gabriel Marcel coined the word 'existentialism'. Its backdrop was: <sup>[1][30][31][32][33][34]</sup>

- the Second World War
- the Nazi death camps
- the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki

Who counts as an existentialist is disputed. The word names no single set of beliefs. Most thinkers grouped under it never used it, or rejected it. Only Sartre and Beauvoir openly claimed it. In 1947 Heidegger rejected any link between his thought and Sartre's existentialism. Yet both encyclopedias we draw on, Stanford's and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, still count him as a key figure. Camus gave a 1945 interview titled, in English, 'No, I am not an existentialist'. Even so, the Stanford encyclopedia places his essay The Myth of Sisyphus squarely in the tradition. <sup>[35][36][1][20][9]</sup>

## Key works

- **Fear and Trembling** (Søren Kierkegaard, 1843): Retells the story of Abraham and Isaac to ask what faith can demand beyond ordinary morality. <sup>[5][15][28]</sup>
- **The Gay Science** (Friedrich Nietzsche, 1882; expanded 1887): Also published in English as The Joyful Wisdom. Poses a famous test: would you live your life again, forever? <sup>[37][16][38]</sup>
- **Being and Time** (Martin Heidegger, 1927): Humans as always caught up in a shared world, lost in 'the They' until anxiety calls them back. <sup>[31][30][17]</sup>
- **The Myth of Sisyphus** (Albert Camus, 1942): Asks whether life is worth living. Its image is Sisyphus, pushing his rock up a mountain, again and again, forever. <sup>[21][39][9]</sup>
- **Being and Nothingness** (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1943): The movement's defining book, on our absolute freedom, bad faith and 'the look' of others. <sup>[40][41][42][43]</sup>
- **Existentialism Is a Humanism** (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1945 lecture, published 1946): A packed Paris lecture, an accessible version of Being and Nothingness. <sup>[32][44]</sup>
- **The Second Sex** (Simone de Beauvoir, 1949): How women are cast as man's 'Other', and man as the essential being. A founding text of second-wave feminism, the women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s. <sup>[45][23][46]</sup>

## Main criticisms

In Germany, critics such as Adorno called existentialism obscure, abstract and even mystical, Heidegger above all. Many other philosophers agreed. After Marxist criticism in the 1940s and 1950s, Sartre himself decided his early idea of 'radical' freedom, made by choice alone, was too abstract. It ignored the social conditions that limit real choices. The French philosopher Merleau-Ponty objected that no one is free apart from other people and history. Later French thinkers faulted Sartre for neglecting language and the impersonal structures that shape meaning. <sup>[47][43][23][48]</sup>

Other criticisms are moral and political. Critics said existentialists neglected social and political structures, with dangerous results. Heidegger is notorious for his involvement with the Nazis in the 1930s. Nietzsche's ideal of the self-creating individual is openly elitist and anti-democratic. The Nazis exploited his work, a use the Internet Encyclopedia calls 'utterly misdirected'. Camus accused Kierkegaard, Jaspers and Heidegger of escaping into 'forced hope'. He meant turning to God, or something beyond this life, to escape the absurd. Some argue the label does more harm than good, lumping very different ideas together. <sup>[47][36][16][49][18][35]</sup>

## Why it still matters

Existentialism reached far beyond philosophy, into: <sup>[1][29][50]</sup>

- films by Ingmar Bergman and Jean-Luc Godard
- theatre, above all the plays of Beckett
- painting, from Edvard Munch to Edward Hopper
- the politics of Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X
- the writing of Richard Wright and Ralph Ellison
- feminism, through Beauvoir
- the book Black Skin, White Masks by Fanon, on colonised Black people
- psychotherapy, in the work of Viktor Frankl, Rollo May and Irvin Yalom

Among philosophers, verdicts differ. The Internet Encyclopedia finds the movement as a whole has had relatively little direct influence there. Yet Nietzsche and Heidegger remain very much alive. It adds that the movement made emotion a serious subject of research. The movement also insisted that philosophy deal directly with real-life topics such as sex and death. The Stanford encyclopedia sees the movement thriving, and its courses are among philosophy's most popular. In its view, the movement has shaped: <sup>[47][51][52][53][54][55][56]</sup>

- later French thinkers such as Foucault and Derrida
- theories of the self as a life story
- the science of the mind, which now sees the mind as rooted in the body
- medicine and nursing

## Apply it to your life

Existentialists do not look at life from a cool distance. They start from a person caught up in living. Kierkegaard wanted 'a truth which is true for me', lived and felt rather than just known. Their ideas also shaped existential psychotherapy, which helps people with questions such as meaning and the fear of death. One example is logotherapy, a therapy built around finding meaning, invented by the Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl. <sup>[4][25][29][57][58]</sup>

### Notice who you chose to ask

*From: Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism is a Humanism*

For existentialists, no god and no absolute moral law can guide our choices or justify them. We alone are responsible, and realising that often brings anxiety. As Sartre put it, 'there are no excuses behind us nor justifications before us'. <sup>[2]</sup>

**Try this.** Sartre's own example is a student of his in wartime France. The student's brother had been killed in the German attack of 1940. He could go to England and fight with the Free French Forces. Or he could stay with his mother, who lived only for him. He came to Sartre for advice. But choosing an adviser, Sartre said, is already a choice: you more or less know what they will say. His only reply was 'You are free, therefore choose, that is to say, invent.' One way to use his point: before your next hard choice, ask yourself why you picked the person you are asking, and what you already expect them to say. <sup>[59]</sup>

Themes: Freedom and anxiety, Existence before essence

### Ask what this moment asks of you

*From: Viktor Frankl, logotherapy*

For Sartre and Beauvoir, no fixed meaning is handed to us. Beauvoir said existence is not absurd but ambiguous: 'its meaning is never fixed'. A 2015 review pooled the results of 15 trials of existential therapies. Meaning-focused therapies had large effects on people's sense of meaning. It found most support for structured programmes that discussed meaning directly with physically ill patients. But the studies were few and low in quality. That is evidence for therapy, not for reflecting alone. <sup>[14][60][61]</sup>

**Try this.** Frankl held that we discover meaning rather than make it. He turned the usual question round: we should not ask what life means, but recognise 'that it is he who is asked'. In therapy, logotherapists often use Socratic dialogue: guiding a person by asking questions. They help clients find meanings for themselves. The Viktor Frankl Institute of Logotherapy names three avenues to meaning. One way to borrow that on your own: this week, ask yourself how each avenue showed up for you. <sup>[58][62][63][64][65][66]</sup>

- What you gave, through work or deeds.
- What you received, through experiences or relationships.
- The stance you took, especially towards suffering you could not avoid.

Themes: Meaning without guarantees

### Would you live it all again?

*From: Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science §341*

For Nietzsche, the key to giving your life its own style is radical acceptance. That means saying yes to your whole life, the good and the bad. He called this attitude amor fati: love of one's fate. He also thought only 'the highest types' have that kind of freedom. <sup>[16]</sup>

**Try this.** Imagine a demon tells you that you must live this life again, 'innumerable times', with nothing new in it. Would you curse the demon? Or would you answer that you never heard anything more divine? If the thought took hold of you, Nietzsche wrote, it would transform you, or perhaps crush you. The question 'Dost thou want this once more, and also for innumerable times?' would lie as the heaviest burden on everything you do. One influential reading treats it as a test of whether your life has been good. He meant your life as a whole, in every detail. If that feels too big, one way in (our adaptation, not his): ask it of a single day first. <sup>[67][68]</sup>

Themes: Authenticity versus the crowd, Meaning without guarantees

> This is for reflection, not therapy. If anxiety or low mood feels heavy, please talk to a GP or a mental health professional.

## Study this school

> Teach me Existentialism as a short Socratic module: ask me one question at a time, and finish with a five-question quiz. Start with what it is: a loose movement that begins from real, lived human life. It is rooted in Kierkegaard and Nietzsche and was made famous by Sartre and Beauvoir in mid-20th-century France. Note that Camus and Heidegger rejected the label. Then cover its six themes: <sup>[1][12][3][10][7][8][15][9][36]</sup>
>
> - existence before essence
> - freedom and anxiety
> - authenticity versus the crowd
> - meaning without guarantees
> - my freedom needs yours
> - faith and the leap
