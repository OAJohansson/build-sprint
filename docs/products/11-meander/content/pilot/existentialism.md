# Existentialism: content pilot

*Philosophy that starts from the single person who must choose how to live. <sup>[1][2][3]</sup>*

## In one minute

Existentialism is a loose family of ideas, not a tidy school. It starts from life as a person lives it, not from the detached, objective standpoint that much earlier philosophy preferred. A core idea: we make ourselves through our choices, and we are responsible for who we become. <sup>[1][4]</sup>

Its ideas began to come together in the nineteenth century, with Søren Kierkegaard, from Denmark, and the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche. The movement took off in mid-twentieth-century France, where Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, two French philosophers and novelists, made it famous. <sup>[5][1][6][7][8][9]</sup>

Who counts? Of the major figures, only Sartre and Beauvoir called themselves existentialists. The French novelist Albert Camus and the German philosopher Martin Heidegger both rejected the label, but both are still counted in the tradition. Existentialists can be religious, like Kierkegaard, a Christian, or not, like Nietzsche and Sartre. <sup>[10][11][12][13][1][14][15]</sup>

It rejects fixed moral rules, but it does not say that morality is empty. A good life is still possible: one where we own our choices and help others be free too. <sup>[1]</sup>

## It asks

1. If nothing hands me a purpose, how do I live? <sup>[5][16][12]</sup>
2. Am I living my own life, or just doing what everyone else does? <sup>[17]</sup>
3. What do I do with the anxiety of being free to choose? <sup>[3][18]</sup>

## Themes

### Existence before essence

*Your choices shape who you become, within real limits.*

Sartre called it 'the first principle of existentialism': existence precedes essence. Your 'essence' would be a fixed nature that decides who you are; existentialists say we are not born with one. A person first 'turns up' and only afterwards defines himself, through choices made as life unfolds. How free are we, then? Early Sartre called our freedom 'absolute'. Later, he and others such as Beauvoir stressed that our circumstances, such as our body and our time and place, limit it. But we can still question those limits, give them meaning and decide what to do with them. A strong craving, for example, does not force me to act on it. <sup>[19][1][20]</sup>

### Freedom and anxiety

*You alone answer for your choices, and that can feel dizzying.*

This makes us free, and responsible for what we make of ourselves. Take Sartre's example of a coward. Childhood or genes did not make him that way; he 'makes himself a coward' by his decisions. Realising this brings anxiety, which Kierkegaard called 'the dizziness of freedom'. Existentialists ask us to face this feeling, not run from it. It can shake us out of our comfortable routines. <sup>[3][21][18][2]</sup>

### Authenticity versus the crowd

*Going along with everyone feels comforting, but it lets us dodge our own choices.*

Being authentic means being true to yourself. Existentialists criticise our habit of living by the rules and expectations of everyone around us. Heidegger's name for this crowd was 'the They': we enjoy, read and judge things as 'they' do. It feels like living well, but it hides our freedom from us. <sup>[5][17][1]</sup>

Sartre and Beauvoir call this kind of self-deception 'bad faith'. It comes in two forms: <sup>[17][22]</sup>

- Treating yourself as a fixed thing: 'I am what I am', and nothing can change me.
- Pretending you have no limits, as if your past and your situation did not constrain your choices.

What authenticity looks like varies by thinker: <sup>[14][23][24][22]</sup>

- Kierkegaard: a passionate commitment that unifies a scattered life.
- Nietzsche: creating your own values and saying yes to your whole life.
- Heidegger: pulling your life together around what matters to you, while staying ready to let it go if your circumstances change.
- Sartre and Beauvoir: facing your unsettled situation clearly and taking responsibility for what you do.

### Meaning without guarantees

*As the old religious answers faded, three thinkers asked how life can still mean something.*

As nineteenth-century Europe grew less religious and more scientific, the old picture that gave life moral direction began to collapse, leaving nihilism: the shocking sense that our existence has no overall reason or purpose. Three thinkers answered it differently: <sup>[1][5][25][23][26][10][27][28][29][22]</sup>

- Nietzsche summed up the crisis as 'God is dead'. He did not mainly mean that there is no God. He meant that belief in the Christian God had become unbelievable, so the morality built on it would collapse. His answer: create your own values.
- Camus called our situation 'the absurd', the clash between our 'wild longing for clarity' and 'the unreasonable silence of the world'. If life has no meaning, he asked, is it still worth living, or is suicide the answer? He said it is worth living: reject suicide and false hope, and live fully aware.
- Beauvoir disagreed with calling life absurd: that would mean it can never have a meaning. Its meaning is never fixed, so we keep making it.

### My freedom needs yours

*Other people can trap us as objects, yet my freedom depends on theirs.*

Sartre and Beauvoir argued that we see ourselves through 'the look' of others, and that their gaze can turn us into objects. Sartre's example: you are peeping through a keyhole, lost in the scene. Suddenly you hear footsteps, and you feel shame as you see yourself as an object in someone else's eyes. Sartre's play No Exit sums this up in its famous line that hell is other people. <sup>[30][31]</sup>

But there is a better way. Beauvoir's 'authentic love' nurtures the other person's freedom instead of possessing them. Why does my freedom need yours? We see ourselves through each other's eyes, so my freedom is bound up with yours. That is why we must help free each other, so that each of us can create ourselves. As Sartre put it, in wanting freedom 'we discover that it depends entirely on the freedom of others'. <sup>[30][16]</sup>

### Faith and the leap

*For religious existentialists, faith is a personal commitment reason can't justify.*

Kierkegaard prized 'a truth which is true for me', lived and felt rather than proved. In Fear and Trembling he retells the Bible story of Abraham: <sup>[32][33][14]</sup>

- God tells Abraham to kill his son Isaac.
- Abraham is ready to do it, which is disturbing: as a father, he has a moral duty to protect his son.
- At the last moment God intervenes and saves Isaac.

Kierkegaard's point: for a person of faith, a personal truth can count for more than ordinary morality, even when reason cannot make sense of it. Committing to it is a 'leap': a choice no argument can justify, which cuts you off from what everyone else thinks right. The obvious worry: what if someone excused violence as a command of God? <sup>[14][33]</sup>

## Where it came from

In mid-twentieth-century France, the movement's backdrop was the Second World War, the Nazi death camps and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The key works, in order: <sup>[1]</sup>

- 1843 · **Fear and Trembling** (Søren Kierkegaard): The story of Abraham and the 'leap' of faith. <sup>[6][14]</sup>
- 1882 · **The Gay Science** (Friedrich Nietzsche): Also translated as The Joyful Wisdom. Home of 'God is dead' and of the test of living your life again, forever. <sup>[34][35][25][23]</sup>
- 1927 · **Being and Time** (Martin Heidegger): How we lose ourselves in 'the They', and how anxiety can call us back. <sup>[36][24]</sup>
- 1942 · **The Myth of Sisyphus** (Albert Camus): An essay on the absurd. It ends with Sisyphus, condemned in myth to roll a rock up a mountain forever. Facing that fate fully aware makes it his own, which is why Camus says 'One must imagine Sisyphus happy'. <sup>[37][28][10]</sup>
- 1943 · **Being and Nothingness** (Jean-Paul Sartre): The groundwork of the movement in France: freedom, bad faith and 'the look'. <sup>[38][39][40][31]</sup>
- 1945 · **Existentialism Is a Humanism** (Jean-Paul Sartre): A packed Paris lecture, an accessible version of his ideas. Part of what he meant by 'humanism': we have no lawmaker but ourselves. <sup>[8][41]</sup>
- 1949 · **The Second Sex** (Simone de Beauvoir): How society casts woman as 'the Other': defined only in relation to men, who count as the essential ones. <sup>[42][43][38]</sup>

## Main criticisms

Two common criticisms, and two troubling facts about its thinkers: <sup>[44][20][11][23]</sup>

- It can be obscure: German critics called it abstract, even mystical, Heidegger above all.
- It paid too little attention to social and political structures. Sartre later agreed that his early idea of 'absolute' freedom left these out.
- Heidegger is notorious for his involvement with the Nazis in the 1930s.
- Nietzsche's ideal is openly elitist and anti-democratic: he thought only a very few people could be free.

## Why it still matters

Where it left its mark: <sup>[38][1][44]</sup>

- Feminism: The Second Sex became a founding text of second-wave feminism, the women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s.
- Politics: its stress on freedom and on the struggle to create yourself informed the liberation politics of Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X.
- Philosophy: it made emotions a serious subject of study, and insisted philosophy deal directly with real-life topics such as sex and death.

## Apply it to your life

Existentialist ideas shaped existential psychotherapy, which aims to help people with questions such as meaning and the fear of death. Even thinkers whose views differed from the existentialists' took up these questions, such as the Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, who created logotherapy, a therapy built around finding meaning. <sup>[45][46][47][48][22]</sup>

### Notice who you chose to ask

*From: Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism is a Humanism*

Sartre put it starkly: 'there are no excuses behind us nor justifications before us'. <sup>[2]</sup>

**Try this.** Sartre's own example is a student of his in wartime France, who came to him for advice. He could: <sup>[41]</sup>

- go to England and join the Free French Forces, to avenge his brother, killed in the German offensive of 1940, or
- stay with his mother, who lived only for him.

But choosing an adviser, Sartre said, is already a choice: you more or less know what they will say. His only reply was 'You are free, therefore choose, that is to say, invent.' One way to use his point: before your next hard choice, ask yourself why you picked the person you are asking, and what you already expect them to say. Either way, the choice stays yours.

Themes: Freedom and anxiety, Existence before essence

### Ask what this moment asks of you

*From: Viktor Frankl, logotherapy*

Sartre and Beauvoir say we make meaning through our choices. Frankl held the opposite: we discover meaning, and cannot make it. A 2015 review found that meaning-focused therapy strengthened people's sense of meaning. Its studies were few and low in quality, and they tested therapy, not reflecting alone. <sup>[49][50][22][48]</sup>

**Try this.** Frankl also turned the usual question round. We should not ask what life means, he said: 'each man is questioned by life'. We answer by taking responsibility for our own life. Frankl named three avenues to meaning. One way to borrow that on your own: this week, ask yourself how each avenue showed up for you. <sup>[51][52]</sup>

- What you gave, through work or deeds.
- What you received, through experiences or relationships.
- The stance you took, especially towards suffering you could not avoid.

Themes: Meaning without guarantees

### Would you live it all again?

*From: Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science*

Nietzsche called the attitude of saying yes to your whole life, the good and the bad, amor fati: love of one's fate. You do not have to share his elitism to try his question. <sup>[23][53]</sup>

**Try this.** Imagine a demon tells you that you must live this life again, 'innumerable times', with nothing new in it. Would you curse the demon? Or would you answer that you never heard anything more divine? If the thought took hold of you, Nietzsche wrote, it would transform you, or perhaps crush you. One influential reading treats it as a test of whether your life has been good, as a whole and in every detail. If that feels too big, one way in (our adaptation, not his): ask it of a single day first. <sup>[54][55]</sup>

Themes: Authenticity versus the crowd, Meaning without guarantees
