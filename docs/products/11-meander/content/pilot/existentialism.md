*Philosophy that starts from the single person who must choose how to live. <sup>[1][2][3]</sup>*

## In one minute

Existentialism is a loose family of ideas, not a tidy school. It starts from life as a person lives it, not from a detached viewpoint. One of its core ideas is that we make ourselves through our choices, and we are responsible for who we become. Two nineteenth-century thinkers laid the groundwork: Søren Kierkegaard, from Denmark, and the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche. It became famous in mid-twentieth-century France through Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, two French philosophers who also wrote novels. <sup>[1][4][5][6][7][8]</sup>

It rejects fixed moral rules, but it does not say that morality is empty. A good life is still possible: one where we own our choices and help others be free too. <sup>[1]</sup>

## It asks

1. If nothing hands me a purpose, how do I live? <sup>[9][10][11]</sup>
2. Am I living my own life, or just doing what everyone else does? <sup>[12]</sup>
3. What do I do with the anxiety of being free to choose? <sup>[3][13]</sup>

## Themes

### Existence before essence

*Your choices shape who you become, within real limits.*

Sartre called it 'the first principle of existentialism': existence precedes essence. Your 'essence' would be a fixed nature that decides who you are. Existentialists say we are not born with one. A person first 'turns up' and only afterwards defines himself, through choices made as life unfolds. Sartre adapted the idea from the German philosopher Martin Heidegger. Hard facts still limit us, such as our body and the time and place we live in. But we can question those facts, give them meaning and decide what to do with them. A strong craving, for example, does not force me to act on it. <sup>[14][1]</sup>

Thinkers: Jean-Paul Sartre

### Freedom and anxiety

*No one else can make your choices for you, and that can feel dizzying.*

This makes us free, and responsible for what we make of ourselves. Take Sartre's example of a coward. Childhood or genes did not make him that way; he 'makes himself a coward' by his decisions. No god and no absolute moral rule can choose for us or excuse us. Realising this brings anxiety. Kierkegaard called anxiety 'the dizziness of freedom'. Existentialists ask us to face this feeling, not run from it. It can shake us out of our comfortable routines. <sup>[3][15][13][2]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Jean-Paul Sartre

### Authenticity versus the crowd

*Going along with everyone feels comforting, but it lets us dodge our own choices.*

Being authentic means being true to yourself. Existentialists criticise our habit of living by the rules and expectations of everyone around us. Heidegger's name for this crowd was 'the They'. We enjoy, read and judge things as 'they' do. It feels like living well, but it hides our freedom from us. So we are deceiving ourselves. Sartre and Beauvoir call this self-deception 'bad faith'. It comes in two forms: <sup>[9][12][16][1]</sup>

- Treating yourself as a fixed thing: 'I am what I am', and nothing can change me.
- Pretending you have no limits, as if your past and your situation did not constrain your choices.

What authenticity looks like varies by thinker: <sup>[17][18][19][16]</sup>

- Kierkegaard: a passionate commitment that unifies a scattered life.
- Nietzsche: creating your own values and saying yes to your whole life.
- Heidegger: a firm commitment you stay free to give up.
- Sartre and Beauvoir: facing your unsettled situation clearly and taking responsibility for what you do.

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir

### Meaning without guarantees

*Without God or a built-in purpose, meaning has to be made, not found.*

As nineteenth-century Europe became less religious and more scientific, the old religious picture that gave life moral direction began to collapse. Nietzsche diagnosed the result: nihilism, the shocking sense that our existence has no overall reason or purpose. His image for this loss was 'the death of God'. The French novelist Albert Camus called our situation 'the absurd'. It is the clash between our 'wild longing for clarity' and 'the unreasonable silence of the world'. Camus asked whether such a life is worth living. His answer was yes: the absurd calls for action, not suicide. Beauvoir insisted that existence is not absurd but 'ambiguous'. Calling it absurd, she said, denies that it can ever be given a meaning. Calling it ambiguous says 'its meaning is never fixed', so we keep making it. <sup>[1][9][20][21][22][16][23][24]</sup>

Thinkers: Friedrich Nietzsche, Albert Camus, Simone de Beauvoir

### My freedom needs yours

*Other people can trap us as objects, yet my freedom depends on theirs.*

Sartre and Beauvoir argued that we see ourselves through 'the look' of others. Their gaze can turn us into objects. Relationships then become a power struggle. Sartre's play No Exit sums this up in its famous line that hell is other people. But there is a better way. Beauvoir's 'authentic love' nurtures the other person's freedom instead of possessing them. Why does my freedom need yours? If I value my own freedom, I must also want it for others. As Sartre put it, in wanting freedom 'we discover that it depends entirely on the freedom of others'. <sup>[25][10]</sup>

Thinkers: Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir

### Faith and the leap

*For religious existentialists, faith is a personal commitment reason can't justify.*

Existentialism is not only atheist. Kierkegaard was a Christian. He prized 'a truth which is true for me', lived and felt rather than proved. His book Fear and Trembling retells the Bible story in which God tells Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac. Here is the paradox. As a father, Abraham has a moral duty to protect his son. God's command asks him to break that duty. By committing to God's command, Abraham becomes a 'knight of faith': someone who puts a personal truth above ordinary morality, a truth reason cannot make sense of. Nietzsche and Sartre, by contrast, saw God as irrelevant or even harmful to our deepest choices. <sup>[1][26][17][27][28]</sup>

Thinkers: Søren Kierkegaard, Friedrich Nietzsche, Jean-Paul Sartre

## Where it came from

Existentialism became a movement in the nineteenth century, as industry and bureaucracy made society feel impersonal. Early voices included: <sup>[9][26][3][29][1]</sup>

- Kierkegaard, who attacked detached philosophy for leaving out the single living person.
- Nietzsche, who foresaw the coming nihilism.
- The Russian novelist Fyodor Dostoevsky. His 'underground man' refuses to be reduced to 'a piano key' by the view that laws of cause and effect decide everything a person does.

In mid-twentieth-century France, its backdrop was the Second World War, the Nazi death camps and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Three dates mark its rise: <sup>[1][30][7][8]</sup>

- 1927: Heidegger's Being and Time, which shaped Sartre and Beauvoir.
- 1943: Sartre's Being and Nothingness. The word 'existentialism' is first used.
- 1945 to 1949: Sartre's lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism and Beauvoir's The Second Sex.

The label 'existentialist' is itself disputed. Of the major figures, only Sartre and Beauvoir openly claimed it. Heidegger and Camus both rejected it. Even so, scholars still place both within the tradition. Some argue the label does more harm than good, because it lumps very different ideas together. <sup>[31][32][1][22][11]</sup>

## Key works

- **Fear and Trembling** (Søren Kierkegaard, 1843): Retells the story of Abraham and Isaac to ask what faith can demand beyond ordinary morality. <sup>[5][17][28]</sup>
- **The Gay Science** (Friedrich Nietzsche, 1882; expanded 1887): Poses a famous test: would you live your life again, forever? <sup>[33][18]</sup>
- **Being and Time** (Martin Heidegger, 1927): Humans as always caught up in a shared world, lost in 'the They' until anxiety calls them back. <sup>[30][34][19]</sup>
- **The Myth of Sisyphus** (Albert Camus, 1942): Its image is Sisyphus, pushing his rock up a mountain, again and again, forever. <sup>[23][35][11]</sup>
- **Being and Nothingness** (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1943): One of the movement's defining books, alongside Beauvoir's work of the period, on our absolute freedom, bad faith and 'the look' of others. <sup>[36][37][38][39]</sup>
- **Existentialism Is a Humanism** (Jean-Paul Sartre, 1945 lecture, published 1946): A packed Paris lecture, an accessible version of Being and Nothingness. In it Sartre argued that our actions define our humanity. <sup>[7][40][32]</sup>
- **The Second Sex** (Simone de Beauvoir, 1949): Argues that women are made to live relative to men, as 'the Other'. A founding text of second-wave feminism, the women's movement of the 1960s and 1970s. <sup>[41][42][43]</sup>

## Main criticisms

Some criticisms target its ideas: <sup>[44][39][42]</sup>

- German critics called it obscure, abstract, even mystical, Heidegger above all.
- Sartre's early idea of freedom ignored the social conditions that limit real choices. Sartre himself later agreed.
- Others objected that no one is free apart from other people and history.

Others are moral and political: <sup>[44][18][20][32]</sup>

- It neglected social and political structures, with dangerous results.
- Heidegger is notorious for his involvement with the Nazis in the 1930s.
- Nietzsche's ideal is openly elitist and anti-democratic: he thought only a few strong people could be free.
- Camus said Kierkegaard, Heidegger and others escaped into 'forced hope'. He meant turning to God, or to something beyond the limits of the human condition, to escape the absurd.

## Why it still matters

Existentialism reached far beyond philosophy: <sup>[1][29][42][45]</sup>

- Its ideas spread into films, novels, plays and paintings.
- Its stress on freedom and self-creation shaped the politics of Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X.
- Through Beauvoir's The Second Sex, it helped shape feminism.
- It shaped psychotherapy, and more recently medicine and nursing.

Within philosophy, it left its mark too: <sup>[44][46]</sup>

- It made emotion a serious subject of study.
- It insisted that philosophy deal directly with real life, such as sex and death.
- Nietzsche and Heidegger are still widely studied.
- Its courses are often among philosophy's most popular.

## Apply it to your life

Existentialist ideas shaped existential psychotherapy, which helps people with questions such as meaning and the fear of death. One example is logotherapy, a therapy built around finding meaning. It was created by the Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl. <sup>[29][47][48]</sup>

### Notice who you chose to ask

*From: Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism is a Humanism*

As we saw, no god or moral law can choose for us. Sartre put it starkly: 'there are no excuses behind us nor justifications before us'. <sup>[2]</sup>

**Try this.** Sartre's own example is a student of his in wartime France. He could go to England and fight with the Free French Forces. Or he could stay with his mother, who lived only for him. He came to Sartre for advice. But choosing an adviser, Sartre said, is already a choice: you more or less know what they will say. His only reply was 'You are free, therefore choose, that is to say, invent.' One way to use his point: before your next hard choice, ask yourself why you picked the person you are asking, and what you already expect them to say. <sup>[49]</sup>

Themes: Freedom and anxiety, Existence before essence

### Ask what this moment asks of you

*From: Viktor Frankl, logotherapy*

On meaning, Frankl disagrees with Sartre and Beauvoir. They hold that we make meaning through our choices. Frankl holds that meaning exists for us to discover, and that we cannot make it. A 2015 review of existential therapy trials found that meaning-focused therapy strengthened people's sense of meaning, but the studies were few and low in quality, and they tested therapy, not reflecting alone. <sup>[50][51][16][52]</sup>

**Try this.** Frankl also turned the usual question round. We should not ask what life means, he said: 'each man is questioned by life'. We answer by taking responsibility for our own life. Logotherapists often guide people by asking questions, so that they find meanings for themselves. Frankl named three avenues to meaning. One way to borrow that on your own: this week, ask yourself how each avenue showed up for you. <sup>[53][54][55][56]</sup>

- What you gave, through work or deeds.
- What you received, through experiences or relationships.
- The stance you took, especially towards suffering you could not avoid.

Themes: Meaning without guarantees

### Would you live it all again?

*From: Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science §341*

As we saw, Nietzsche's ideal was saying yes to your whole life, the good and the bad. He called this attitude amor fati: love of one's fate. He thought only 'the highest types' could truly live this way. You do not have to share his elitism to try his question. <sup>[18]</sup>

**Try this.** Imagine a demon tells you that you must live this life again, 'innumerable times', with nothing new in it. Would you curse the demon? Or would you answer that you never heard anything more divine? If the thought took hold of you, Nietzsche wrote, it would transform you, or perhaps crush you. One influential reading treats it as a test of whether your life has been good, as a whole and in every detail. If that feels too big, one way in (our adaptation, not his): ask it of a single day first. <sup>[57][58]</sup>

Themes: Authenticity versus the crowd, Meaning without guarantees
