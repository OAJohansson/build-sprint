# Research brief: does a skeleton before the details help people learn?

Written 26 Nov 2026 for the "framework for learning" idea (feedback #28). Every source below was
found and checked online (title, authors, year, venue); links at the end. Strength labels:
**strong** = several meta-analyses or many replications agree; **moderate** = one good
meta-analysis or a consistent body of experiments with caveats; **thin** = theory, a review of
opinion, or one or two studies.

## In short

- **Yes, an overview before the details helps, but modestly.** Advance organisers ("here is the big
  idea everything hangs on") help learning and retention; meta-analyses put the effect from small
  (d ≈ 0.2) to moderate (≈ 0.6). It's real, not magic.
- **It helps most for newcomers and messy material.** The benefit is largest when the learner lacks
  background and when the material itself is poorly organised, and it shows more on applying ideas
  than on reciting facts. For people who already know the field the extra structure can stop
  helping or even get in the way.
- **Maps of the ideas work, and they work better when the learner builds or edits them.** Studying
  a concept map beats studying plain text (g ≈ 0.4); constructing one does better still (g ≈ 0.7–0.8).
  Maps mainly help people remember the central ideas, much less the details.
- **Learning the names and parts first works.** Brief "pre-training" on the main concepts before the
  full explanation improves understanding (Mayer's pre-training principle, several experiments).
- **Showing "how much is there / how far have I got" does not reliably help.** Evidence is thin
  and mixed: progress bars in online surveys do not reduce drop-out overall, and bad-news progress
  increases it. Timelines help children grasp historical time in one solid study; nothing found for
  adults learning the history of ideas.

## What the evidence says

### 1. Overview or structure before details

**Advance organisers (Ausubel).** A short, more general introduction given before new material, meant
to give the new details something to attach to.

- Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980), 135 studies: organisers had a facilitative effect on both learning
  and retention; ERIC's summary calls the effect "small". The Visible Learning database lists it as
  d = 0.21. **Strong that there is an effect; it is small.**
- Stone (1983), 29 studies: organisers associated with increased learning and retention; listed as
  d = 0.66. **Moderate.**
- Across 10 meta-analyses of advance organisers (901 studies) the Visible Learning database
  (Hattie) gives an average of d = 0.46, with individual meta-analyses ranging from about 0.1 to 0.9.
  Treat the average loosely: it mixes very different studies. **Strong for "positive on average",
  wide spread.**
- The field argued about this: Barnes & Clawson (1975, 32 studies) concluded organisers did not
  reliably help. Mayer (1979, two reviews) replied that the effect depends on conditions (see
  section 2). **The disagreement itself is the useful finding: organisers are not automatic wins.**

**Schema theory.** People understand new information by fitting it into an existing mental frame.

- Bransford & Johnson (1972): a passage that is nonsense on its own ("the procedure is quite
  simple…") becomes understandable and memorable when readers are told the topic first (washing
  clothes). Telling them the topic *after* reading helped much less. A famous, often-replicated
  demonstration. **Strong as a demonstration that context first matters; it is a lab finding with
  short texts.**

**Elaboration theory (Reigeluth).** Start with an "epitome", a simple whole view of the subject,
then zoom in on parts and zoom back out to the whole, like a zoom lens.

- Reigeluth & Stein (1983), a chapter in an instructional design book. It is a design
  prescription built on Ausubel and schema theory. We found few direct controlled tests of the
  full theory. **Thin as evidence; useful as a design pattern.** It matches the owner's idea almost
  exactly (skeleton, then details, then reconnect).

**How experts organise knowledge.** National Research Council, *How People Learn* (2000), a
consensus report: experts' knowledge is organised around core concepts or "big ideas", not lists of
facts, and so curricula should be organised to build that kind of understanding. **Strong as an
expert consensus; it describes experts and recommends, it doesn't test an overview screen.**

**Cognitive load: pre-training.** Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002), three experiments: learners who
first got a short introduction to each part and its possible states did better on transfer tests
in all three experiments and on retention in two, than learners who went straight to the full
explanation. Mayer later named this the pre-training principle ("people learn more deeply… when
they know the names and characteristics of the main concepts"). **Moderate:** replicated by Mayer's
group, mostly with short science and mechanics lessons, not philosophy.

**Concept and knowledge maps.** Node-and-link diagrams of ideas and how they relate.

- Nesbit & Adesope (2006), 55 studies, 5,818 learners: using concept maps was associated with
  better retention, overall mean effect 0.60. Studying a map: 0.37; constructing one: 0.82.
  Studying a map vs. text: 0.39; vs. outlines or lists: 0.28. **Strong.**
- Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018), 142 effect sizes, 11,814 learners: overall g = 0.58;
  creating maps g = 0.72, studying maps g = 0.43; worked in STEM and non-STEM subjects. **Strong**
  (it is a newer, larger update by the same group, which is a mild caveat).

### 2. What makes an overview work, and what doesn't

- **The learner's prior knowledge.** Mayer (1979, Instructional Science, 44 studies) tested
  Ausubel's predictions: organisers should help more for learners who lack the background, more for
  poorly organised material, and more on transfer than on rote recall; his review supported
  these predictions. **Moderate.**
- **Too much help for people who know it already.** The expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga, Ayres,
  Chandler & Sweller, 2003): guidance that helps novices becomes redundant, and sometimes harmful,
  as learners gain expertise. **Strong** (many experiments, mostly in maths and technical
  domains). Implication: the skeleton should be skippable or shrink for people who know the field.
- **Learner-built beats given.** Constructing maps outperforms studying them (0.82 vs 0.37 in
  Nesbit & Adesope; 0.72 vs 0.43 in Schroeder et al.). **Strong.** But building a map from scratch
  vs. writing an outline is a much smaller gap (0.19), so the gain comes from active work on the
  structure, not from the map format itself.
- **Big ideas, not details.** Studying maps instead of text helped recall of central ideas (0.60)
  much more than detail ideas (0.20) (Nesbit & Adesope 2006). The authors note maps may be biased
  toward summary. **Moderate.** A skeleton helps people hold the shape; the details still need
  their own learning.
- **Visual vs text.** Maps beat plain text somewhat (0.39) and beat outlines/lists less (0.28).
  **Moderate.** A visual helps, but a clear structured list gets you most of the way.
- **Signalling.** Cues that make the organisation of the material visible (headings, highlighting,
  arrows) help: retention g = 0.53, transfer g = 0.33 (Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey 2018,
  103 studies), and reduce cognitive load. Prior knowledge did not change the effect. **Strong.**
  This is the strongest support for "keep connecting details back to the skeleton visibly".
- **Order matters.** In Bransford & Johnson, context given after the text helped far less than
  before. In Mayer et al. (2002), post-training did worse than pre-training. **Moderate.**

### 3. Showing scope and progress ("how big, how far have I got")

**Thin, and mixed.** We found no good learning studies; the nearest evidence is from surveys and
marketing.

- Villar, Callegaro & Yang (2013), meta-analysis of 32 web-survey experiments: a steady progress bar
  did *not* significantly reduce drop-out. Bars that moved fast at first reduced drop-out; bars that
  moved slowly at first increased it. **Moderate for surveys, transfer to learning unknown.**
- Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006): people speed up as they near a goal (coffee-card stamps), and an
  "illusionary" head start increases effort. **Moderate for purchases, untested for learning here.**
- Our read: showing how big a subject is may help orientation (that is the advance organiser
  effect), but there's no evidence that a size or progress meter by itself keeps people going, and
  a big, slow number may put people off.

### 4. Timelines and chronology

**Thin.** One solid study: de Groot-Reuvekamp, Ros & van Boxtel (2018) found that teaching with
"Timewise", a curriculum built around a layered classroom timeline with a small number of named
eras, improved 6–12-year-olds' understanding of historical time. Children, not adults, and
historical time, not history of ideas. Many teacher guides recommend timelines, but that is
practice wisdom, not evidence. We found nothing testing timelines for learning schools of thought.

## What this means for Meander

1. **Show the skeleton before the details, and keep it short.** Advance organisers and pre-training
   both help when given *first*, and help less after (Luiten 1980; Stone 1983; Bransford & Johnson
   1972; Mayer et al. 2002). One screen: the few big ideas, the core question, when and where.
2. **Make the skeleton the core ideas and thinkers, named.** Pre-training works by giving the names
   and roles of the main parts (Mayer et al. 2002); experts organise around big ideas (*How People
   Learn* 2000). The skeleton is "here are the 4 ideas and 5 people you'll meet", not a table of
   contents.
3. **Pitch it at the newcomer, and let people who know it skip.** The benefit is biggest for
   learners without background (Mayer 1979); for experts the same help can hurt (Kalyuga et al.
   2003). Default on for a first visit, collapsible or skippable later.
4. **Let the learner place things on the skeleton themselves.** Building or editing a map beats
   reading one (Nesbit & Adesope 2006; Schroeder et al. 2018). Cheapest version: after a detail
   card, ask "which of the big ideas does this belong to?" or "who is this closest to?" and show the
   answer on the skeleton.
5. **Keep pointing back to the skeleton as details arrive.** Signalling is one of the strongest
   effects here (Schneider et al. 2018): a small "you are here" marker or highlight on the skeleton
   for every detail.
6. **A simple structure is enough to start; a diagram is a bonus.** Maps beat text, but only a
   little over a good outline (0.28; Nesbit & Adesope 2006). Ship a clear list or simple map first,
   test a richer visual later.
7. **A short timeline with a few eras is reasonable, but it's a bet.** Layered timelines with few
   named eras helped children (de Groot-Reuvekamp et al. 2018); untested for adults and ideas. Treat
   "where it sits in time" as an assumption to user-test.

**What the evidence warns against**

- **Don't expect the skeleton to teach the details.** Maps and overviews help central ideas far more
  than details (0.60 vs 0.20; Nesbit & Adesope 2006). Details still need their own practice.
- **Don't lean on a size or progress meter for motivation.** There's no learning evidence, and in
  surveys a steady bar did not help while a slow-start bar made drop-out worse (Villar et al. 2013).
  "40 ideas, 12 hours" up front could scare people off; test before shipping big numbers.
- **Don't overload the skeleton.** Its value is giving novices a small frame to hang things on; an
  exhaustive overview turns into the very load it was meant to reduce (cognitive load theory,
  Kalyuga et al. 2003).
